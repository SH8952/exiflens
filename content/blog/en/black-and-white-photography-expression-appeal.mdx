In a color photo, color is the first thing the eye notices. In a scene with strong color, such as a red sunset or a blue sea, color can even become the subject. Remove the color from the same scene, though, and the story changes. The eye lingers on lines and shapes, on how light or dark things are, and on how rough or smooth a surface looks, and the photo takes on a quieter but firmer feeling.

This article goes through what makes black and white photography distinctive, using 7 photos finished in monochrome and the shooting settings (EXIF) of each. It covers a corridor lined with concrete columns, a pier stretching out over the sea, columns reflected in a puddle, a lighthouse and chimneys against the sky, people sitting under a tree, and an alley where eaves and a stone wall overlap, and looks at what makes each scene work in black and white.

## What Remains When You Take the Color Away

Shooting in black and white is not simply removing color. It is closer to rereading a scene so that other elements take over the job color used to do. In a color photo, color contrast pulls the eye, but in black and white that role passes to differences in brightness, lines, shapes, and texture. The table below lists the elements that become especially important in black and white, with an example from the photos in this article.

| Element | What it does in black and white | Example in this article |
|---|---|---|
| Lines and perspective | Create a path for the eye to follow | The corridor of columns and steel beams |
| Symmetry and repetition | Create rhythm and order | The pier with two pavilions, columns reflected in water |
| Tonal contrast | Separates the subject from the background | The lighthouse and chimneys against the sky |
| Silhouette | Conveys mood through shape alone | The tree and the people on benches |
| Texture | Gives a feeling you could almost touch | Rough concrete, a stone wall, wooden pillars |

When color disappears, distracting elements are tidied up along with it. The primary colors of signs and the busy colors of a background no longer stand out, leaving only shape and brightness, so even a complicated scene reads more calmly. The reverse is also true: a scene with weak shapes or no difference in brightness tends to look flat once it is converted. Which scenes suit black and white is covered separately below.

## Lines and Perspective: Structure Appears When Color Disappears

The photo at the top is the scene with the most lines in this article. On the left are thick concrete columns, above are steel beams crossing at regular intervals, and the floor narrows toward an arched opening at the far end. In color, the eye might have gone first to the colors of faded concrete and rusty steel, but in black and white the lines made by beams and columns stand out clearly as the skeleton of the frame. Because all of those lines converge on the arch, the eye is naturally drawn inward.

The settings were 35mm, F5.6, ISO 50, 1/125 s. At 35mm, the long end of the RF15-35mm, the corridor keeps its sense of depth while the edges stretch less than at an extreme wide angle like 15mm, and stopping down to F5.6 kept everything from the rough floor in front to the arch far away reasonably sharp. Lowering ISO to 50 keeps noise low even in the dark faces of the columns, so the tones stay smooth. For how wide-angle and telephoto lenses change perspective in a scene like this, see the [wide-angle vs. telephoto focal length guide](/en/guides/wide-angle-vs-telephoto-focal-length).

The reason black and white works so well for a scene full of lines is that the thickness and direction of the lines become the composition itself. If you pick one converging line and give the eye a destination (here, the arch), the whole photo reads like a single path. Composition using lines is also covered in [5 Composition Checks Before You Press the Shutter](/en/blog/camera-composition-five-checks-before-shutter).

## Symmetry, Repetition, and Reflection: Scenes That Become Graphic

Black and white gains strength from repeated shapes and symmetry. Without color, the rhythm that the same shape makes when it repeats feels clearer. Both photos below use repetition of form in a vertical frame.

The photo on the left was taken standing in the middle of the pier so that the frame is left-right symmetrical. The path starts at the bottom of the frame and converges to a point, and at its end two identical pavilions balance each other. The sky and sea are both pale gray, so most of the frame is quietly empty, and only the dark forms of the pier and pavilions stand out clearly within it. In a symmetrical composition, tilting the camera to one side or shifting slightly off center breaks the balance easily, so it helps to turn on gridlines and check the horizon and the center.

The photo on the right shows columns and the horizontal pipes above them repeating in the same pattern. The dark columns continue at regular intervals and are mirrored on the wet floor, so the same form appears twice, above and below. In color, rusty tones and sky color might have split the eye's attention, but in black and white the columns and their reflections look almost like pure geometry. In a reflection photo the exposure of the reflected area can change a lot depending on how bright the sky reflected in the floor water is, so it is safest to set exposure while checking on the screen that the bright parts are not blown out.

Both photos use 35mm and F5.6. In scenes where a form has to stay sharp far into the distance, stopping down somewhat is usually an advantage, and with a wide-angle lens around F5.6 is normally sharp enough from the foreground to the distance. If you want to calculate the range in focus in numbers, see the [guide to understanding depth of field](/en/guides/understanding-depth-of-field).

## Contrast and Silhouettes: Leaving Only Light and Shadow

The most dramatic expression in black and white may well be the silhouette. When the sky is bright and the subject is dark, the subject loses its detail but gains a clearly outlined, single shape. In color, the color of the sky can interfere with the outline, but in black and white a bright sky is simply a bright backdrop that makes the shape stand out more.

The photo on the left looks up at a lighthouse from a low position under a cloudy sky. The lighthouse, the tall mast beside it, and the string lights are cut out dark against the light gray sky, and the rough texture of the rocks and stairs below anchors the frame firmly. Exposure was set so the sky does not blow out, so the lighthouse naturally went dark, and that gave the photo a heavier feeling. Setting exposure for the sky and letting the subject go dark like this is the basis of a black and white silhouette.

The photo on the right shows two chimneys against an evening sky when the sun was low. Pulling the two chimneys in with a 70mm telephoto made the gap between them and the difference in their heights the central elements of the frame. The sky is a gradient, dark at the top and lighter toward the bottom, so smooth tonal transitions are visible even in black and white, and power lines crossing the frame add diagonal structure. The leaves in front are blurred at F3.2 and fill the bottom of the frame with a dark, textured band. With the light fading, I raised ISO to 1000 to secure 1/60 s. In black and white, color noise disappears and only grain remains, so this level of ISO can even read as a film-like mood.

For a silhouette to work, the outlines of the subjects should not overlap. In the chimney photo the two chimneys stand apart, so each shape reads on its own, and in the lighthouse photo the radar mast is separate from the lighthouse, so they are distinguished as different shapes. When you are unsure how to set exposure, the [guide to reading a histogram](/en/guides/how-to-read-a-histogram) makes it easy to check whether highlights are blown out, and the [guide to using exposure compensation](/en/guides/using-exposure-compensation) helps when you need to adjust.

This horizontal photo is a silhouette with people added. The tree, with its widely spread branches, reads as one large mass rather than individual leaves, and the two people sitting on the bench beneath it remain only as small shapes. With no detail visible, the viewer reads the mood from their posture and the direction they face toward the sea rather than from expressions or clothes. The composition puts a pier and boat on the right and the sea on the left, with the tree large on the left so the weight leans to one side, and the bright sky and distant horizon on the right balance it.

## Texture and Detail: Surfaces You Can Almost Feel

One of the things black and white shows best is texture. Without color, whether a surface is smooth or rough remains only as subtle differences in brightness, so surfaces such as stone, wood, and aged concrete feel much more real.

This photo pulls in an alley where eaves, a stone wall, wooden pillars, and a utility pole overlap, using a telephoto lens. Different textures are gathered in one frame: the surface of a wall built with rounded stones, the grain of a wooden door, and the stripes of roof tiles. In color, the colors of signs and wires might have scattered the eye, but in black and white they all settle into surfaces of different brightness, and the brightest areas (the sky and white wall at the upper left) become the points that lead the eye.

The settings were 70mm, F2.8, ISO 100, 1/400 s. The R7 has an APS-C sensor rather than full frame, so 70mm corresponds to about 112mm in full-frame terms (a 1.6x conversion), and that angle of view compresses distance so the wall, eaves, and utility pole overlap in one frame. Opening to F2.8 creates a difference in focus between the near stone wall and the far buildings for a sense of depth, and the fast 1/400 s shutter keeps the texture crisp without blur. If you are curious about the conversion, see the [guide to sensor size and crop factor](/en/guides/sensor-size-and-crop-factor-explained).

In scenes where texture matters, the direction of light greatly affects the result. When light comes in at an angle from the side, bumps on the surface appear as shadows and the texture comes alive, while even frontal light makes it look flat. A scene split between light and shadow, like this photo, is a good condition for rich texture in black and white.

## Scenes That Suit Black and White, and Scenes That Don't

Not every scene improves in black and white. The table below lists when a photo comes alive after conversion and when it tends to lose its force.

| Aspect | Scenes that suit black and white | Scenes where black and white disappoints |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Architecture and structures with clear lines, shapes, and repetition | Scenes with vague, monotonous forms |
| Brightness | Scenes with a large difference between light and dark areas | Flat light on an overcast day with similar brightness everywhere |
| Color | Scenes where color is busy and scatters the eye | Scenes where color contrast is the subject, such as sunsets, autumn leaves, and flowers |
| Texture | Subjects with grain, such as stone, wood, aged surfaces, and clouds | Scenes with only smooth, textureless surfaces |

When you cannot decide, it helps to switch briefly to black and white on the camera screen or in an editing program. If the eye still has somewhere to rest and you can feel differences in brightness after the color is gone, the scene is worth finishing in black and white. If the scene was good because of its color, converting it will leave it feeling empty. How different retouching can change the mood of a color photo is also shown in [Same Photo, Different Mood: The Moment Retouching Changes a Photo's Intent](/en/blog/same-photo-different-mood-retouching).

## Points for Shooting and Editing Black and White

A few things to remember from shooting through finishing can change the result of a black and white photo.

First, shooting RAW is better when possible. Even if you shoot with the camera's monochrome mode, the RAW file keeps the color information, so you can change the way it is converted later in an editing program. The difference between RAW and JPEG is covered in [RAW vs. JPEG: Which Should You Shoot?](/en/guides/raw-vs-jpeg-which-should-you-shoot).

Second, use per-color brightness controls when converting. When you convert a color photo to black and white, brightness is determined by the original color, and by moving the per-color sliders in the black and white mixer of an editing program you can darken blue tones (the sky) to make clouds stand out, or lighten red and orange tones (skin, rusty surfaces) to change the mood. The same photo takes on a completely different expression depending on which colors you leave bright.

Third, be conscious of contrast. Black and white looks more powerful the wider the range from the brightest to the darkest areas. If you push contrast too far, though, detail in both dark and bright areas disappears at once, so check that the histogram is not pushed against one end. How to fit dark and bright areas into one frame is covered further in [Understanding Dynamic Range](/en/guides/understanding-dynamic-range).

Fourth, try accepting noise as grain. The color noise that is distracting in color photos disappears in black and white, and only luminance noise remains. Grain at around ISO 1000, as in the chimney photo in this article, can even add a film-like mood. It is better to keep noise modest in places where it stands out, such as faces or smooth sky.

## Checklist

- Is there a line, shape, or texture in the scene for the eye to rest on even without color?
- Is the difference between the brightest and darkest areas large enough?
- When making a silhouette, did you set exposure for the sky so the subject comes out dark?
- Do the outlines of the subjects overlap and merge into a single mass?
- When using symmetry or repetition, did you check the horizon and the center?
- If possible, did you shoot RAW so you can choose the black and white conversion later?

## Common Mistakes: Thinking That Removing Color Is Enough to Make a Black and White Photo

The most common mistake is thinking that simply removing saturation from a color photo makes a black and white photo. When the separation that color provided disappears, two surfaces of different colors can become the same gray and the form turns to mush. If a photo is going to be finished in black and white, choosing scenes by brightness difference and form from the time of shooting leads to much better results.

Another is raising contrast without limit. Strongly contrasted black and white is dramatic, but detail in both bright and dark areas can vanish. Adjust while checking whether there are parts you want to keep, such as clouds in the sky or texture in the shadows. Finally, be careful about expecting black and white to improve by itself in the flat light of an overcast day. A scene with no difference in brightness is flat in black and white too, so in that case you need to carry the photo with other elements such as lines or texture.

## FAQ

### Is it better to shoot with the camera's monochrome mode or convert later?

Turning on the monochrome mode while shooting is convenient because you can judge composition and tones while seeing the black and white result on the screen. If you also shoot RAW, the color information remains and you can convert again later with per-color brightness controls, so using both methods together is the most flexible.

### What exposure is best for black and white photos?

Setting exposure for the highlights so the sky and bright areas do not blow out makes it easy to create silhouettes and contrast. If you want to keep detail in dark areas, you can also give a bit more exposure and pull the bright areas down in editing.

### Can you get good black and white photos on overcast days?

When light is flat and differences in brightness are small, black and white can easily look flat as well. However, in a scene where the cloudy sky becomes a bright backdrop and the subject in front of it becomes a dark silhouette, an overcast day can actually suit black and white.

### How high can ISO go in black and white photos?

In black and white, color noise is not noticeable, so a higher ISO is easier to accept than in color. Grain around ISO 1000, as in the chimney photo in this article, can suit the mood, but noise can be distracting on faces or smooth sky, so decide after checking the result.

## Putting It Together

The appeal of black and white photography is that lines, symmetry, tonal contrast, silhouettes, and texture step in where color used to be. The 7 photos in this article each showed a different element: the lines and perspective of a corridor, the symmetry and reflections of a pier and columns, the silhouettes of a lighthouse, chimneys, and a tree, and the texture of eaves and a stone wall. When choosing a scene to finish in black and white, first ask whether it is good because of its color or because of its form and brightness. Then set exposure to protect the bright areas, and adjust contrast only as far as the details you want to keep do not disappear, and the structure of the photo will come through much more clearly.
