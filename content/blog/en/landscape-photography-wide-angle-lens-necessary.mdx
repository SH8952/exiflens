I think many people, myself included, assume landscapes are shot with a wide-angle lens. In this post I want to explain how landscape photos are captured differently with wide, standard, and telephoto lenses.

But when I picked the photos for this post and checked their EXIF, 6 of the 8 turned out to be full-frame equivalent 41–77mm. They were landscape photos taken without a wide-angle lens. Let's follow the 8 photos below and go through the differences between lenses.

## Why a Wide-Angle Lens Is Treated as the Default for Landscapes

People prefer wide-angle lenses to fit a lot into one photo. On full frame, a 15mm lens has a diagonal field of view of about 111 degrees, 24mm about 84 degrees, 50mm about 47 degrees, and 85mm about 29 degrees. Even standing in the same spot, a wide angle fits a much broader scene into one frame.

This photo brings the sky, clouds, horizon, waves, and rocks all into one frame. It is easy to include a near foreground and a far sky together, and at the same distance and the same aperture, the shorter the focal length, the deeper the depth of field, which also makes it easier to get the front and back sharp. Add the fact that landscapes are broad scenes, and it is natural that the formula "landscape = wide angle" appears.

However, when you fit a lot into one photo, it is easy to lose your intent. As elements in the frame increase, the eye scatters in many directions, and what the photo is trying to show becomes blurry.

This is a city seen from above. Since the sky, sun, buildings, and roads all fit in, if you don't decide what the main subject is, the eye has nowhere to go. The burden of tidying up unneeded objects that come in at the edges grows too.

Of course there are clear situations where a wide angle is right: a narrow space where you can't step back, when you want both sky and ground in one frame, or when you want to emphasize the sense of distance between foreground and background. And if your purpose in shooting isn't necessarily to put in some intent, but simply that it looks pretty or to keep how it looked at the moment you shot, there's no problem at all with capturing it wide.

## Wide, Standard, Telephoto: How Scenery Gets Captured

| Type | Full-frame equivalent focal length | Diagonal field of view (examples) | What it does in landscapes | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wide angle | about 35mm and below | 15mm: about 111° / 24mm: about 84° | Wide space and foreground and background in one frame | Many elements, so intent easily blurs |
| Standard | about 35–70mm | 50mm: about 47° | Natural composition close to what the eye sees | You have to choose what to include |
| Telephoto | about 70mm and above | 135mm: about 18° | Pick part of a distant scene and compress the background | Watch for shake, secure shutter speed |

The dividing lines differ a little from person to person. If you wonder what angle of view your lens has, you can check with the [Camera Field of View Calculator](/en/tools/camera-fov-calculator). The criteria for choosing wide vs. telephoto are also summarized in the [Wide-Angle vs. Telephoto Lens Guide](/en/guides/wide-angle-vs-telephoto-focal-length).

## The Focal Length Shown on a Photo vs. the Equivalent Focal Length

Comparing the focal length written in EXIF as is can cause misunderstanding. Three of the photos in this post were shot with the Canon EOS R7, which has an APS-C sensor, and with APS-C you multiply the displayed focal length by about 1.6 to get the same angle of view as full frame. The same RF15-35mm lens used at 15mm on the R5 Mark II is an ultra-wide, but photos ③ and ④, used at 31–35mm on the R7, are standard angles of view at 50–56mm equivalent. ## Scenery Captured at Standard Angles

The standard angle of view is said to feel similar to the scene as seen by eye. Because the frame doesn't open all at once the way a wide angle does, the photographer decides first what to include and what to leave out.

## What Changes Past 70mm

As the angle of view narrows, less comes into the frame, so the background gets simpler, and the sense of distance between background and subject looks compressed closer. In return, you become more sensitive to shake, so you need to secure a generous shutter speed.

### ⑧ 77mm equivalent: Compressing from a distance

When you shoot from far away with an angle close to telephoto, the distance between the buildings and the surrounding trees looks compressed, so the tower feels much bigger and more solid. A telephoto is also a lens that pulls in a distant landscape and compresses it.

## Four Things That Change with the Lens for the Same Scenery

- **Angle of view**: decides whether you capture wide or pick out a part.
- **Perspective**: a wide angle makes the distance between foreground and background feel large; a telephoto looks compressed.
- **Depth of field**: at the same distance and the same aperture, the shorter the focal length, the deeper the depth of field. The [Hyperfocal Distance Guide](/en/guides/hyperfocal-distance-explained) makes this easier to understand.
- **Tidying the composition**: with a wide angle you have to watch the edges; with a telephoto, shake and shutter speed.

## Checklist

- Have you settled in one sentence what you want this photo to show?
- Are there no unneeded objects coming in at the edges of the frame?
- Is there something that can serve as a foreground? (leaves, rocks, a railing, etc.)
- If you step back or forward a step or two from where you stand, wouldn't there be a better composition?
- If you use a telephoto, have you secured a shutter speed that avoids shake?

## Common Mistake: Wide Angle Because It's a Landscape

If you lock in a wide angle just because it's a landscape, you easily end up with a photo with too many elements where what you shot is blurry. Conversely, shooting only distant subjects with a telephoto can lose the broad feeling of a landscape. It is better not to choose the lens first, but to decide the intent you want to capture and then pick the angle of view that fits.

## FAQ

### Can I use a telephoto lens for landscapes?

Yes. It is actually advantageous for picking part of a distant landscape and showing it with the background compressed. Photos ⑦ and ⑧ in this post are examples.

### If I use only one lens, which angle of view is good?

It depends on the scene you shoot. Of the 8 photos in this post, 4 were shot with the 15-35mm zoom, 3 with the 24-70mm zoom, and 1 with the 70-200mm zoom. Just as the same 15-35mm lens was a wide angle on a full-frame body and a standard angle on an APS-C body, it is best to choose looking at the lens and body combination together.

### How should I read focal length on an APS-C camera?

The displayed focal length multiplied by about 1.6 is the full-frame equivalent focal length. Even the same lens gives a different angle of view depending on sensor size.

### When is a wide-angle lens really necessary?

In a narrow space where you can't step back, when you must fit both sky and ground into one frame, or when you want to emphasize the sense of distance between foreground and background.

## Putting It Together

A landscape doesn't mean a wide angle is the right answer. Wide is good for capturing broadly, standard is good for showing one scene naturally, and telephoto is good for picking only the part you need and compressing it. Before choosing a lens, decide first what you want to show with this photo.
