The alleys of a neighborhood waiting for redevelopment are worth recording even after the people have left, because old signs, brick walls, and faded gates are still there. But shooting such an alley at midday turns out to be surprisingly tricky for exposure. The sky and white walls are blinding, while the foot of a wall and the narrow inside of the alley are dark, so a single frame holds both very bright and very dark areas.

This post contains eight photos taken around noon on June 9, 2026, in a redevelopment-designated alley in the Songnim-dong area of Incheon, along with the EXIF shooting settings of each one. By the time I visited, all residents had already moved out and demolition had not yet started. The recorded shooting times of all eight photos fall between 11:41 a.m. and 12:29 p.m., the hours when sunlight is strongest. The first three photos have color retouching, and the other five are unretouched originals. Let's look at the numbers: how different the brightness inside and outside the alley was under the same sun, and how aperture and shutter speed changed accordingly.

## Why Is Exposure Hard in a Midday Alley?

On an overcast day or at dusk, light spreads softly and the gap between the bright and dark parts of a scene is small. Direct midday sun, on the other hand, widens the gap between surfaces the light reaches and surfaces in shadow. A camera can record only a limited range of brightness at once, so if you expose for the sky, the inside of the alley sinks into black, and if you expose for the alley, the sky blows out to white. What that limit is gets a fuller explanation in [Understanding Dynamic Range](/en/guides/understanding-dynamic-range).

Converting the settings of these eight photos to an ISO 100 exposure value (EV100) makes the difference visible as a number. EV100 combines aperture, shutter speed, and ISO into one number, and assuming the exposure was set correctly, a larger number means a brighter scene. The EV100 of the eight photos ranged from 12.0 to 14.9, a difference of about 3 stops (8 times). It was the same sunlight at the same time of day, yet the scene brightness varied this much between inside and outside the alley. For reference, the "Sunny 16" rule known as the reference for a sunny day outdoors (f/16, 1/100 s, ISO 100) is about EV100 14.6. You can check this reference yourself with the [Sunny 16 Calculator](/en/tools/sunny-16-calculator).

The sixth photo is the front of a two-story building with eye-catching red walls and a blue roof. The settings are 8mm, f/4.5, 1/450 s, ISO 100, with an EV100 of about 13.2. The sky takes up about the top third of the frame, and it is bright, close to white. In a frontal shot like this, the sky has a large share, which makes it easy for the camera to underexpose, and then the ground-floor windows and door of the building turn dark. Expose for the building instead, and the sky blows out. To see how much of the sky's brightness remains, it helps to build the habit of checking the right edge of the histogram, as explained in [How to Read a Histogram](/en/guides/how-to-read-a-histogram). The one on the left is a black-and-white photo looking into the alley through a dark round gap, and the one on the right is a color photo taken head-on from inside the alley.

## What to Know When Shooting with a Compact Camera

Photos 3 through 8 were taken with a FUJIFILM FinePix F60fd. The lens field in the EXIF is empty, and the focal lengths are recorded as 8mm and 18mm. These numbers are the lens's actual focal lengths, and because a compact camera has a small sensor, its full-frame equivalent focal length is a much larger value than the printed number. In other words, even at the same 8mm, the angle of view is completely different from 8mm on a full-frame camera. The relationship between sensor size and equivalence is explained in [Crop Sensor vs Full Frame](/en/guides/sensor-size-and-crop-factor-explained), and you can calculate it yourself in the [Crop Factor Calculator](/en/tools/crop-factor-calculator).

## The Order for Setting Exposure in a Midday Alley

Here is the order I took from these eight photos, written as general shooting tips.

1. First check that the brightest parts, such as the sky or a sunlit white wall, are not blowing out to white. Bright areas that have blown out are hard to bring back with retouching.
2. Keep ISO at its lowest and choose an aperture within f/4 to f/8. At midday the shutter speed is fast enough in this range, 1/250 s or faster, so there is little worry about shake even when handholding. All eight of these photos were also 1/250 s or faster.
3. Even if the inside of the dark alley feels too dark, it is safer to protect the sky and lift the shadows in post-processing than to raise the exposure to save the sky. RAW, which holds plenty of information, helps here, and the difference is explained in [RAW vs. JPEG: Which Should You Shoot?](/en/guides/raw-vs-jpeg-which-should-you-shoot).
4. In scenes with a large dark area or a large sky, the camera pulls the exposure to one side, so check once more with exposure compensation or the histogram.

## Safety Rules for Shooting in a Redevelopment-Area Alley

You may feel the urge to record a neighborhood before demolition, but safety and consideration for residents come first. The photos in this post are a record taken before demolition started, and that does not mean the place still looks the same today. Please check beforehand whether access is possible.

- **Do not enter restricted areas.** Stay away from areas with construction notice fences or no-entry signs, and from sections where demolition has begun.
- **Do not go inside vacant houses or abandoned buildings.** Old buildings can collapse or drop objects, and there are many hazards such as broken glass, exposed rebar, and nails.
- **Shoot from the street and respect private property and remaining belongings.** Even after residents have moved out, the houses and belongings have owners, so do not touch or take anything.
- **Stop shooting or ask permission if residents or workers are present.** Avoid shots that show the faces of people who still live there or of demolition workers, or the inside of homes.
- **Watch for vehicles and construction equipment.** Narrow alleys block your view, and equipment and vehicles move around near demolition zones.
- **Prepare for midday heat and UV.** There is little shade and the reflected heat is strong, so bring water and take breaks. If possible, do not go alone, and let someone know you are visiting.

If you are concerned about location data (GPS) in your photos, see [GPS Data in Photos: What It Reveals and How to Protect Your Privacy](/en/guides/gps-data-in-photos-privacy) so you can remove it before sharing.

## Checklist

- Did you check with the histogram that the brightest parts, such as the sky or white walls, have not blown out?
- With ISO at its lowest, is the shutter speed still fast enough at 1/250 s or faster?
- In scenes with a large dark area or a large sky, has the exposure not been pulled to one side?
- Did you shoot the same place from different distances and angles, as a wide view, a middle distance, and a detail?
- Are you shooting from a street where access is allowed, after confirming it is safe?

## Common Mistake: Exposing for the Dark Inside of the Alley and Blowing Out the Sky

If you raise the exposure because the inside of the alley looks dark, the sky and sunlit walls blow out to white. In a midday alley, you get better results by protecting the brightest area first and lifting the dark areas in retouching. Conversely, if you only watch the sky, the inside of the alley sinks into black, so first decide which of the two is the center of the photo.

## FAQ

### What should I check first when a midday photo comes out too bright or too dark?
Look at the right edge of the histogram first. If the graph is stuck to the right edge, the bright areas have blown out, so lower the exposure. If it is stuck to the left edge, the dark areas have sunk into black.

### Is it best to keep ISO as low as possible at midday?
When the sun is strong, the shutter speed is fast enough even with a low ISO, so there is no reason to raise the sensitivity. The R5 Mark II photos here were taken at ISO 50 and the compact camera photos at ISO 100.

### Does shooting in black and white make midday exposure easier?
The principle of setting exposure is the same. However, without color, contrast decides the photo, so it becomes more important to check whether the clouds in the sky or the texture of the walls remain.

### Can an old compact camera take photos like these?
At midday with plenty of sunlight, ISO 100 and a shutter speed of 1/250 s or faster produced sharp photos. However, because the sensor is small, the amount of information in dark areas differs from a full-frame camera.

## Putting It Together

A midday alley is tricky to expose because sky and shade come into the same frame. In these eight photos, the scene brightness varied by up to about 3 stops within the same time of day. Protect the sky first, keep ISO low, and in scenes with a large dark area, check that the exposure is not pulled to one side, and you can capture a midday alley reliably. In an alley slated for demolition, safety comes first, so record it from a street where access is allowed, with consideration for those around you.
