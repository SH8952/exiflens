A light display on an autumn night looks quite different to your eyes than it does in a photo. The soft glow you saw on site often turns into blown-out white once it passes through the camera, or the whole frame ends up dark and flat. This post records how to choose shutter speed and aperture on a night like that, using photos I actually took.

It contains six photos of a night light exhibition taken on the evening of October 11, 2024, along with each photo's shooting settings (EXIF). The first three are wide-angle shots of whole scenes at f/11 with 1/2 s, 8 s, and 25 s exposures, and the last three are telephoto close-ups of sculptures at f/2.8. Every photo was taken with the camera fixed on a tripod. The point of this post is that on the same night, under the same lights, your settings change a lot depending on which lens you use and how you approach the subject.

## I Shot a Night Light Exhibition in Two Ways

There are roughly two kinds of scenes worth shooting at a light exhibition. One is a wide scene that takes in the path, the sculptures, the streetlights, and the people walking by. The other is a close-up that fills the frame with the shape or color of the light from a single sculpture. The settings these two need are almost opposite. A wide scene has to be sharp across the whole frame, so you close down the aperture and keep the shutter open correspondingly longer. A close-up opens the aperture to blur the background and keeps the shutter relatively short.

That evening I first shot the wide scenes with a wide-angle zoom, then switched to a telephoto zoom and took close-ups of the sculptures. The time between the first and last photo is about 42 minutes. Below I go through, in order, which numbers I picked and why.

The first photo is a wall sculpture drawn in neon lines. The shutter speed is 1/2 s, the shortest of the three wide-angle shots in this post. Neon is a bright light source in its own right, so even with a dark surrounding it exposes brightly in a short time. Thanks to that, the red roof lines and blue wave lines kept their color instead of clumping into white. The red reflection spreading across the ground at the bottom also survived at this exposure.

Closing down to f/11 keeps the focus even across the whole frame, from the flower bed in front of the wall to the sculpture itself. The sculpture is close to flat and doesn't need much depth of field, but when you include the flower bed in front, it is safer to keep the aperture closed down. The second photo is a view of the promenade at 15mm with an 8 second shutter speed. On the APS-C sensor of the R7, 15mm is about 24mm in full-frame equivalent, which was a good angle for fitting the curving path, the lit trees on both sides, and the rows of lights in the distance into one frame. Because the shutter stayed open for 8 seconds, the people walking in the distance blurred into faint shapes and nearly vanished. That is why the path looks quiet even in a busy exhibition. The third photo is the longest exposure in this post, 25 seconds. Purple and orange light falls on the large upward-leaping fish sculpture, and the color of the lights spreads softly across the ground of the round, flower-ringed bed. A person who was near the roof-shaped sculpture on the left moved during the 25 seconds and remains only as a faint trace, while the trees and the sculpture stay sharp without any shake. The longer the shutter, the more clearly still things are recorded and the more blurred moving things become.

These three photos are not a comparison of one scene with only the shutter changed. Each scene had a different sculpture brightness and a different level of darkness around it, so the shutter speed needed was different too. What stayed the same is that I kept the aperture at f/11 and ISO at 100 and adjusted only the shutter to match the brightness. With aperture and ISO fixed, the shutter is the only variable for matching brightness, so you are less likely to get lost while shooting at night.

## How Many Stops Does a Longer Shutter Speed Make?

| Comparison | Shutter speed | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Neon wall → promenade | 1/2 s → 8 s | 4 stops (16×) |
| Promenade → fish sculpture | 8 s → 25 s | about 1.6 stops (about 3.1×) |
| Neon wall → fish sculpture | 1/2 s → 25 s | about 5.6 stops (about 50×) |

One stop is a doubling of the amount of light, and changing the aperture or ISO by one step at the same shutter speed makes the same one-stop difference. Using this relationship, you can convert to aperture or ISO when the shutter gets too long.

| Aperture (ISO 100) | Shutter speed for the same brightness |
|---|---|
| f/11 | 25 s (actual shot) |
| f/8 | about 12 s |
| f/5.6 | about 6 s |
| f/4 | about 3 s |
| f/2.8 | about 1.6 s |

The table above is calculated from the 25 second exposure. If you don't need people to blur or a starburst from the streetlights, it means you can open the aperture and cut the exposure time a lot. You can check this kind of conversion right away by entering the numbers into the [Exposure Stops Calculator](/en/tools/exposure-stops-calculator), and overall night scene settings are also covered in the [Night Cityscape Photography Settings guide](/en/guides/night-cityscape-photography-settings).

## Why f/11, and the Downside of Closing Down Too Far

Closing down to around f/11 for a night long exposure improves three things. The range of focus across the whole frame gets deeper, point light sources like streetlights split into star shapes, and the longer shutter smooths the flow of people and water. All three wide-angle photos here were taken at f/11, which gets those three effects together.

However, closing down too far can reduce sharpness because of a phenomenon called diffraction. f/11 is close to where this effect starts to appear, so you may see a slight loss of sharpness when you enlarge the photo. For wide scenes, choosing between f/8 and f/11 gives a good balance, and if you don't specifically need the starburst, starting near f/8 is also an option. The principle of diffraction and where it starts for each sensor are covered in the [Diffraction and Optimal Aperture guide](/en/guides/diffraction-and-optimal-aperture).

## Tripods and Managing Shake in Long Exposures

Exposures of 8 or 25 seconds can't be shot handheld, so a tripod is essential. All of the photos that evening were also taken with the camera on a tripod. Even on a tripod, the tiny vibration from pressing the shutter button can show up in the photo, so a 2-second timer, a remote, or a smartphone-connected shutter is usually used as well. On windy days, hanging a bag from the tripod's center hook to add weight is a common trick.

The concrete steps for reducing shake are covered in [How to Avoid Camera Shake in Long Exposures](/en/guides/avoiding-camera-shake-long-exposure), and criteria for choosing a tripod are in [How to Choose a Tripod](/en/guides/how-to-choose-a-tripod), so please refer to them together. During a long exposure the sensor heats up and noise can increase, and the settings related to that are covered in [Low-Light Noise Reduction Camera Settings](/en/guides/low-light-noise-reduction-settings).

From here I switched to the 70-200mm telephoto zoom. 70mm is about 112mm in full-frame equivalent, and it gathers several flower and butterfly sculptures into one frame. The aperture is wide open at f/2.8 and the shutter speed is 1/6 s. Getting the same brightness at f/11 would take about 2.7 seconds, so opening the aperture wide cut the exposure time by a large margin.

Opening the aperture thins the range in focus, and the lights lined up above and behind the sculptures soften into blobs of light. The small lights in the distance became round dots that give the frame depth, and the sculptures separate from the dark background and look crisp. Because a telephoto has a narrow angle of view, it naturally removes unneeded elements from the background, which also helps when you want to pick out just the parts you want in a busy exhibition.

## Bright Lights: Set Exposure So Highlights Don't Blow Out

LED and neon lights glow alone in a dark background, so when the camera tries to match average brightness, the lit parts tend to become too bright. After shooting, check the highlight warning (blinking bright areas) and the histogram on the screen, and if the lights clumped into white, it helps to reduce exposure by one or two stops and shoot again. Dark areas can be recovered later in editing, but blown-out areas are hard to restore.

In a light exhibition with many colors, white balance also changes the impression of the photos a lot. If you leave it on auto, the color shifts from scene to scene and the set loses unity when you place photos side by side, so it is easier to fix it to one value or shoot RAW and adjust later. The principle of white balance is covered in [Understanding White Balance](/en/guides/white-balance-explained).

## Checklist

- Did you first decide whether to capture the whole scene or fill the frame with a single sculpture?
- Did you set the tripod firmly and press the shutter with a 2-second timer or a remote to reduce shake?
- Did you choose aperture and shutter depending on whether you want people to blur or want a starburst from the lights?
- After shooting, did you check the histogram and highlight warning to make sure the lights didn't blow out to white?
- Did you fix white balance to one value or shoot in RAW?

## Common Mistake: Not Setting Exposure for the Bright Lights

The most common mistake is not setting exposure for the brightly glowing lights. Just because it is a dark night, if you keep the shutter long and the aperture wide open, neon and LEDs quickly blow out to white. When highlights blow out to white, the color information in that part disappears with them, so protecting the bright areas comes first. Don't trust the brightness of the preview screen at first; judging by the histogram is the safer approach.

The second mistake is touching the tripod during a long exposure or letting it shake in the wind. Even tiny movements are recorded over 8 and 25 seconds, so don't touch the tripod while shooting, and press its center down with the weight of a bag. When you aim for the blur of people, don't just wait for moments with almost no one around; a moment with someone walking slowly past leaves a faint trace that adds atmosphere to the scene.

## FAQ

### How should I choose the aperture for night light festival photos?

If you want to capture a wide scene sharply and turn streetlights into star shapes, close down to around f/8-f/11. If you want to fill the frame with a single sculpture and blur the background, open up to a value like the lens's maximum aperture of f/2.8. In these photos too, the wide scenes were shot at f/11 and the telephoto close-ups at f/2.8.

### How many seconds should I shoot to make people blur?

It depends on how fast people walk, but once you pass a few seconds, shapes begin to smear faintly. In these photos, people walking in the distance almost disappeared at 8 seconds, and at 25 seconds a person nearby remained only as a faint trace. Even for the same duration, people closer to the camera tend to blur more.

### Can I shoot a light exhibition without a tripod?

A light exhibition is dark, so shooting handheld means keeping the shutter speed short, which means opening the aperture or raising ISO. To shoot sharply with long exposures or low ISO as in these photos, a tripod is the most reliable method. If you don't have one, you can try resting the camera on a railing or wall and using a 2-second timer.

### What ISO should I use for long exposures?

If you can fix the camera on a tripod, leaving ISO at the lowest value (ISO 100 for all of these photos) and matching brightness with the shutter speed is better for noise. Only when the shutter becomes too long should you open the aperture or raise ISO one step at a time.

## Putting It Together

A night light exhibition becomes clearer to set up when you split your approach into a wide-angle long exposure of the whole scene and a telephoto close-up of the sculptures. For the wide scenes I kept f/11 and ISO 100 and adjusted brightness and the blur of people with the shutter speed (1/2 s, 8 s, 25 s), and for the telephoto close-ups I used f/2.8 and 1/6 s to blur the background and keep the detail of the lights. Either way, the key is to fix the camera on a tripod and check the highlights so the bright lights don't blow out to white. At the next light exhibition, try shooting the same place once with a wide angle and once with a telephoto.
