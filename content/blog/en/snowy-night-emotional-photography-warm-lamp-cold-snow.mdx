On a snowy night you cannot help taking the camera out. Snowflakes drifting down under a streetlight, a pure white road that nobody has walked, branches holding a little snow: each of these becomes a mood with a single frame. Yet when you actually shoot them, the snow often melts into blurry dots, or the whole frame comes out dark and dull. The mood snow creates changes a great deal with how you set exposure and color temperature, so sorting out the settings once makes the next snowy day much easier.

This post contains eight photos and their shooting settings (EXIF) from the early hours of February 2, 2026: some taken where snow was still coming down heavily, and some taken in another place after it had stopped and settled. The one shot taken lowest to the ground was retouched toward blue to give a cold mood, and the other seven were given warmer retouching on top of the streetlight glow. We will look at, with numbers, how the same snow can look cold or warm depending on the direction of retouching. All eight photos were shot at 35mm with an RF35mm F1.4 L VCM on a full-frame Canon EOS R5 Mark II.

## 12:52 a.m., While the Snow Was Still Coming Down

The shoot began while the snow was still falling. The first photo is the back of a person filming the snow with a phone on a road lined with streetlights.

The settings are f/1.4, 1/60 s, and ISO 500. Converted to EV100 that is about 4.6, a scene more than 10 stops darker than the clear-day reference value of 15. I opened the aperture fully to take in as much light as possible and raised ISO to 500 to secure 1/60 s. 1/60 s is a speed that makes snowflakes drag slightly into short streaks. In the upper part of the frame the flakes look streaked, while the snow piled on the person's hood and shoulders stays crisp. When you want to keep the sense of snow falling, that is, its motion, this speed suits well.

Now let us see, with two photos, how the snow changes when the shutter speed goes up to 1/250 s in the same place.

Both are f/1.4, 1/250 s, and ISO 640. EV100 is about 6.3, roughly 1.7 stops brighter than the first photo. Because the streetlight was close, raising ISO only to 640 was enough to use 1/250 s. In the photo on the left, snowflakes around the streetlight are frozen as small dots. If the first photo showed the motion of falling snow, this one has the feel of each flake held still in its own shape. Even at the same f/1.4 and 35mm, snow falling nearby becomes big soft discs of light that blur gently across the frame. Included with a figure reaching out a hand, as on the right, the feeling of a snowy day comes across much more easily.

## Same Snow, Different Shutter Speed

The difference among the three is simple to sum up. Near 1/60 s the snow runs in lines, and at 1/250 s or faster it stops as dots. Neither is better; you choose by scene. When snow is pouring down, 1/250 s makes the flakes look more numerous, while for light snow 1/60 s keeps the flowing feel better. 1/60 s is faster than the 1/35 s handheld guideline for a 35mm lens, so it is safe to hold by hand, but a moving person can blur, so when a figure is in the frame it is better to wait for a moment when they stand still.

Working this out beforehand means less fumbling on site. To check the exposure steps yourself, enter values in the [exposure stops calculator](/en/tools/exposure-stops-calculator), and if you wonder how the three EXIF numbers connect, [how to read ISO, shutter, and aperture in EXIF](/en/guides/understanding-exif-iso-shutter-aperture) is a good companion.

## After the Snow Stopped: A Cold Snowfield Shot from the Lowest Point

When the snow had stopped completely, I moved to another place. This time I got low and put the camera close to the snow, and only this frame was retouched toward blue for a cold mood.

It is f/1.4, 1/30 s, ISO 400, with an EV100 of about 3.9. Once the snow stops, no flakes fall from the sky, so a slow shutter speed is less of a burden. 1/30 s is very slightly slower than the 1/35 s handheld guideline for a 35mm lens, but with your body low and the camera braced firmly on the snow, it can be held. The streetlight spreads in a fan across the grain of the snow on the ground and the sky sinks to deep navy, so the same snowfield becomes a cold scene entirely unlike the earlier photos. A low viewpoint magnifies the texture of the ground while pushing the distant streetlight and railing small, which is good for bringing out the stillness of a snowfield.

## Two Ways the Same Snowfield Turned Warm

All the remaining photos were retouched warm. If the cold blue tone is closer to the true color of snow, the warm tone brings out the orange of the streetlights and makes it feel as though the lamp light has settled over the snow.

The photo on the left focuses on a dried flower stalk holding snow, with the background fully softened at f/1.4. EV100 is about 2.6, the darkest of the eight, so ISO went up to 1000. Distant streetlights and lights become round discs of orange and white floating around the frame. Stand one snow-covered stalk sharply and soften the rest, and a small subject looks far more emotional.

The photo on the right puts a snow-covered pine, a red phone booth, and a line of lights in the distance into one frame. In a snowy landscape, just one color entering between white and a dark background gives the picture its center. If everything so far was about gathering light at f/1.4, the last two photos go the opposite way, stopping down to f/10.

As the table shows, exposure on a snowy night clusters between EV100 2.6 and 6.3, which is 9 to more than 12 stops away from a clear day (about 15). Scenes that gather light at f/1.4 adjust ISO and shutter speed a little at a time, while scenes that want star-shaped rays and long exposures stop down to f/10 and then lengthen the shutter speed.

## Exposure and Color on Snowy Days: What to Remember

Snow easily fools a camera's metering. When white snow fills the frame, the camera judges the scene to be bright and darkens the photo, so snow often comes out a dull gray. In that case, raising exposure compensation by about +0.3 to +1 stop brings the white back. At night, as in these photos, the surroundings are dark and exposure can overshoot, so it is safer to check the histogram. Exposure compensation is covered in [how to use exposure compensation](/en/guides/using-exposure-compensation), and the histogram in [how to read a histogram](/en/guides/how-to-read-a-histogram).

Color comes down to white balance. The same snowfield turns blue and cold when you lower the color temperature and yellow and warm when you raise it. In these eight, too, the one blue frame and seven warm ones parted ways in the direction of retouching, not in shooting settings. For the principle, see [white balance explained](/en/guides/white-balance-explained). Shooting RAW makes this kind of color adjustment much freer.

## Preparing and Staying Safe on Snowy Days

Batteries drain faster in cold weather, so keep a spare in a pocket close to your body. Snow that lands directly on the camera and lens melts into water, so a lens hood and a dry cloth give peace of mind. When going from the cold outdoors into a warm room, a lens can fog, so it is safer to leave it in the bag and let it adjust to the temperature slowly. Snowy paths are slippery and dark, so when shooting from a low position or stepping backward, check your footing and surroundings first. On roads or places where people walk, safety comes before the shot.

## Checklist

- Have you decided whether to keep the motion of falling snow or freeze the flakes, and picked a shutter speed between 1/60 s and 1/250 s?
- Did you check the histogram and exposure compensation so the photo does not come out dark because of the white snow?
- Did you think before shooting about whether to retouch toward a warm mood or a cold one?
- Do you have a tripod or something to brace on for scenes that need exposures of 3 seconds or more?
- Did you pack a spare battery and a dry cloth, and check your footing first on slippery ground?

## Common Mistake: Only Raising ISO Because the Snow Comes Out Dark

When a snowy night photo comes out dark, the first urge is to raise ISO. But raising ISO alone increases noise noticeably in the dark sky and in shaded snow and smears the grain of the snow. It is better to follow this order: open the aperture first, slow the shutter as far as the snow does not blur, and raise ISO last. Of these eight photos, six are at ISO 640 or lower and the highest value is only ISO 1250. For ways to reduce noise, see [low-light noise reduction settings](/en/guides/low-light-noise-reduction-settings).

Another is retouching every snowy scene in the same tone. If you apply a warm tone you made once to every photo, even scenes that suit cold stillness start to look awkward. Splitting the work as here, with the low-angle snowfield in blue and the scenes with streetlights in warm tones, keeps the mood rich even within a single post.

## FAQ

### How do I keep snowflakes sharp on a snowy day?

Raise the shutter speed to 1/250 s or faster and pick a spot where a bright background, such as a streetlight, sits behind the flakes, and they freeze as clear dots. The 1/250 s photos in this post are an example.

### How do I make snow flow in lines?

A slow shutter speed around 1/60 s smears flakes into short lines and gives a falling feel. If a person is in the frame, movement can blur them, so wait for a moment when they stop.

### Why does my snow come out gray?

When white fills the frame, the camera judges the scene bright and shoots it dark. Raise exposure compensation by about +0.3 to +1 stop and check the histogram.

### Can I shoot a snowy night without a tripod?

Up to around 1/30 s it is possible if you get low or brace on something firm like a railing, but exposures longer than 3 seconds need a tripod. Stabilization has its limits too.

## Putting It Together

Emotional photos on a snowy day come down to three things: choosing how the snow looks with shutter speed, protecting the white of the snow with exposure compensation and the histogram, and splitting warm from cold with retouching. These eight moved between f/1.4 and f/10 and between 1/250 s and 3.2 s, yet EV100 stayed between 2.6 and 6.3. Even the same snow becomes an entirely different story depending on the time, the viewpoint, and the tone you choose.
