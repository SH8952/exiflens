If you ask travelers to name one neighborhood in Seoul where traditional hanok houses cluster together, many will say Bukchon Hanok Village. In the middle of the city, tiled roofs, narrow alleys, and stone steps continue one after another, and N Seoul Tower can even be seen in the distance between them. This post is written so that international travelers planning a trip to Korea can read it too.

This post contains 12 photos taken on the afternoon of October 8, 2024, together with the shooting settings (EXIF) of each. Ten were taken in the alleys around Bukchon, and two are views from nearby. Alongside the photos, I have also gathered visiting information confirmed as of November 2026 (the hours when tourists may visit, the fine, and how to respect the residents), so please check it before you go.

> **Please read before you shoot.** Portrait rights are protected strictly in Korea, so please compose your photos so that other people's faces are not clearly visible in the frame. In Bukchon, where residents actually live, wait for people to pass or choose a composition in which no one can be identified.

## What Kind of Place Is Bukchon Hanok Village in Seoul?

The name Bukchon (北村) comes from the idea of a village north of Cheonggyecheon Stream and Jongno, and today it is widely used for the area around Gahoe-dong, Gye-dong, and Samcheong-dong, where hanok and narrow alleys remain. According to a November 2024 citizen-reporter article from Seoul Metropolitan Government's Media Hub, the area between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung that covers Sogyeok-dong, Hwa-dong, Anguk-dong, Samcheong-dong, Gahoe-dong, Gye-dong, and Wonseo-dong has been designated by Jongno-gu as a special management area, and about 6,000 residents live there.

That makes Bukchon both a tourist destination and a residential neighborhood where people actually live, unlike a museum or a palace. The doors, windows, and walls you meet in the alleys may all belong to someone's home, and that changes both how you walk the neighborhood and how you take photos. The scenes in the photos below were taken with that in mind.

## Why International Travelers Love It, as Seen in Photos

If you asked why Bukchon is so popular with travelers, every person would answer a little differently, but once you start taking photos the reasons that show up in the frame are fairly clear. The first is that the details of the hanok are close at eye level. The curves of the roof tiles, the rafters lined up under the eaves, and the wooden lattice windows can all be seen simply by walking the alleys. The second is that every alley offers a different scene. Stone steps, stone walls, and every corner you turn bring a new composition. The third is that old rooflines and a modern city appear in the same frame.

The photo above shows N Seoul Tower in the distance between two hanok roofs. It was taken at 70mm, F2.8, ISO 100, 1/500 s, which corresponds to about 112mm in full-frame terms, so the distant tower, hill, and buildings were pulled into one frame while the eaves in front were left blurred to work like a picture frame. Placing part of an eave or roof tiles in the foreground on purpose makes the city look as if it is peeking through the hanok, which gives even a first-time visitor a photo that says Seoul at a glance.

The fourth reason is the everyday traces such as shops and signs. Even the hanok alleys have cafes and small shops, and these signs blend with the old buildings and give the photos a story. ## Visiting Information to Know Before You Go (as of November 2026)

The information below summarizes the Seoul Media Hub article and the Bizhankook on-site report that I checked on November 12, 2026. Rules like these can change, so it is safest to check the Jongno-gu notices once more right before your visit.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Area covered | Around Bukchon-ro 11-gil in Gahoe-dong (the red zone). Bukchon is managed in red, orange, and yellow zones |
| Hours tourists may visit | 10:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. (restricted hours are 5:00 p.m. to 10:00 a.m. the next day) |
| Timeline | Designated a special management area in July 2024, a guidance period from November 2024 to February 2025, and full enforcement from March 1, 2025 |
| Fine | Entering for tourism purposes during restricted hours can result in a fine of 100,000 won |
| Exceptions | People with a clear purpose, such as shop or museum customers and hotel guests, can pass after being checked |
| On-site guidance | "Bukchon Security Guards" provide guidance and warnings, and information on the exceptions is also available through QR codes |
| Residents | It is a place where people actually live, so move quietly and avoid opening gates or looking inside |

The Bizhankook article reports that shop owners asked for the summer visiting hours to be extended to 6:00 or 6:30 p.m., and that Jongno-gu only said it could consider a flexible approach. Nothing has been decided, so for now it is best to plan around 5:00 p.m. For details, see the [Seoul Media Hub article (November 2024)](https://mediahub.seoul.go.kr/archives/2012688) and the [Bizhankook on-site report (August 2026)](https://bizhankook.com/articles/bukchon-tourist-curfew-split-reactions.html).

All the photos in this post were taken on October 8, 2024, so they are a record from before the restricted-hours system began. Still, the 10 photos taken around Bukchon were shot between 3:15 p.m. and 4:23 p.m., which falls within today's visiting hours. The times are the values I checked in each photo's EXIF.

## Tips for Photographing in Bukchon

Most of the 12 photos in this post were taken with the same lens and similar settings, but focal length, aperture, and shutter speed differ by scene. The table below summarizes the settings used in this post by scene.

| Scene | Settings in this post | Point |
|---|---|---|
| Corner hanok, whole alley | 15mm (about 24mm equivalent), F2.8, 1/160 s | Include roof, wall, and sky together and keep the horizon level |
| Eaves and tile details | 70-200mm, F2.8, 1/160-1/800 s | Pull in the layered structure up close |
| Stairs and doors | 70-200mm, F2.8, 1/250-1/400 s | Expose for the bright area and let the dark area fall |
| Signs and shops | 70mm, F2.8, 1/500 s | Focus on the sign and blur the background |
| City view and hanok | 70mm, F2.8, 1/500 s | Place the eaves in front like a frame |

Ten of the 12 photos in this post were taken with the RF70-200mm telephoto lens, most of them at F2.8, and both choices were intentional. First, Bukchon is visited by a great many tourists, so I used a telephoto lens to pull scenes in from a distance and keep people out of the frame. Second, opening the aperture wide blurs the front and back, so the eye is not scattered and gathers on the subject I wanted to show.

Pulling scenes in with a telephoto lens also lets you capture details such as eaves and doors without walking up close to a house, which is easier on the residents too. Still, avoid including the inside of gates or windows, and if a person ends up in the photo, keep the face from being recognizable. The Seoul article also stresses that quiet conversation matters in the narrow alleys, so lowering your voice and the sound of your movement while shooting is the basic courtesy.

There are moments when a wide angle is needed. As in the first photo of this post, where the corner hanok is taken at 15mm, a wide angle is handy when an alley is narrow or when a whole building has to fit in the frame. If you find it hard to pack both lenses, see [Travel Photography: Prime Lens or Zoom Lens?](/en/blog/travel-prime-lens-vs-zoom-lens) for a comparison of which suits a trip.

## Checklist

- Did you check the visiting hours (until 5:00 p.m.) and the latest notices before leaving?
- Did you compose your shots so that the inside of residents' gates and windows is not in the frame?
- Are you avoiding talking loudly or standing in the alley to block the way while shooting?
- When shooting with a telephoto lens, did you secure a shutter speed that matches the equivalent focal length?
- In scenes with a bright sky and dark eaves together, are the highlights still intact?

## Common Mistakes: Going to Shoot Without Checking the Visiting Hours and Residents' Daily Life

The most common mistake is going around sunset without checking the visiting hours. After 5:00 p.m., entry for tourism purposes is restricted and a fine may be imposed, so even if you want to photograph the sunset, it is safer to move your plan to earlier in the afternoon. The next is making noise in front of residents' homes or looking into gates. Remember that a hanok alley is someone's living space before it is scenery.

On the technical side, many people zoom to 200mm and use a slow shutter speed like 1/100 s and end up with blurred photos. Zoom in on the image to check, and raise ISO to increase the shutter speed if needed. Conversely, getting right up to a house with a wide angle can also be a burden on residents, so staying back and using a telephoto lens is easier for everyone.

## FAQ

### Until what time can I visit Bukchon Hanok Village?

According to reports checked as of November 2026, the hours when tourists may visit the red zone (around Bukchon-ro 11-gil in Gahoe-dong) are 10:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. There are exceptions such as shop or museum customers and hotel guests, and the hours may change, so check the Jongno-gu notices before your visit.

### Which lens is good for photos in Bukchon?

In this post, 10 of the 12 photos were taken with a 70-200mm telephoto and 2 with a 15-35mm wide angle. The telephoto was useful for capturing details such as eaves and tiles from a distance, and the wide angle for corners and whole narrow alleys.

### What time of day is best for photos?

The Bukchon photos in this post were taken between 3:00 p.m. and about 4:20 p.m. The clouds in the sky stood out, and the late-afternoon light gave the trees and tiles warm color. Because visiting hours end at 5:00 p.m., I recommend starting early in the afternoon.

### Do Bukchon alleys also suit black and white photos?

The lines and textures made by eaves, stone walls, poles, and wires are clear, so they suit black and white well. A comparison of the same alley in color and in black and white appears in the middle of this post.

## Putting It Together

Bukchon Hanok Village is a place where a single walk lets you meet many scenes: tiles and eaves, stone steps, signs in the alleys, and N Seoul Tower in the distance. At the same time, it is a neighborhood where people live, so it is best to check the visiting hours of until 5:00 p.m. and the manners of moving quietly before you go. For photography, a combination of a telephoto lens for details from a distance and a wide angle for corners and whole alleys worked well. Use the settings in this post as a reference when choosing the lens and time of day that fit your travel plan.
