Food ordered while traveling cools quickly, so there is little time to think about the photo. That makes food photography a shoot where you have to decide within a few seconds from what distance, at what angle, and how close to shoot. This post is a record of those three decisions, organized with real photos and their settings.

It contains 7 photos of food ordered in Thailand on April 30, 2025, with the shooting settings (EXIF) of each. Fried rice, a stir-fried shrimp dish in sauce, and glossy chicken wings are shot in steps from full shots to close-ups, all with the wide-angle lens I had brought for landscape photography. The point of this post is that there is a way to get close even without a dedicated macro lens.

## Shooting Food with a Wide-Angle Lens

The lens for this shoot was the Canon RF15-35mm F2.8 L IS USM, the wide-angle lens I had prepared for landscape photography, and I used it to shoot the food. Standard or short-telephoto angles of view are generally said to be used for food, but that day I simply tried shooting food with that wide-angle lens. One thing I confirmed in the process: every lens has a minimum focusing distance, the closest distance at which it can focus, and if you get this distance right, even a lens without a macro function can shoot as close as possible, almost like a macro shot.

The camera is a Canon EOS R7 with an APS-C sensor, so multiplying the focal length by 1.6 gives the full-frame equivalent angle of view. 26mm corresponds to about 42mm and 35mm to about 56mm, so the angle of view in these photos is closer to standard than to wide. Even on a wide-angle zoom, using the 35mm end gives an angle of view that works comfortably for food, and the distortion that makes the plate edge look bent was not large. You can check the conversion yourself with the [Crop Factor Calculator](/en/tools/crop-factor-calculator).

## The Minimum Focusing Distance Is the Key to Close-Up Shots

The minimum focusing distance is the closest distance at which focus can be achieved, and it is measured not from the front of the lens but from the sensor position inside the camera (the ⌀ mark on the top of the body). So the real gap between the front of the lens and the subject is shorter than the number in the specifications. In Canon's official specifications, this lens's closest focusing distance is listed as 0.28m and its maximum magnification as 0.21x.

A maximum magnification of 0.21x means that the image formed on the sensor is 0.21 times the size of the real subject. The R7's sensor is about 22.3mm wide and 14.8mm high, so calculating from that, the area that fits in the frame at the closest distance is about 10.6cm wide and 7cm high. That is about the size where one or two shrimp and a spoonful of rice fill the frame, and for food photography that is enough to look like a close-up.

| Item | Typical wide-angle or standard lens | Macro lens |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum magnification | Often around 0.1x to 0.3x | Often 1x (life size) |
| Minimum focusing distance | Listed per lens in the specifications | Can get much closer |
| Use in food photography | Whole plate to close-up | Extreme enlargement of small details |
| Advantage | Possible with a lens you already own | Can enlarge even very small subjects |

The values in this table are general tendencies and differ by lens, so it is a good idea to first check the minimum focusing distance and maximum magnification in your own lens's specification sheet. Macro photography as a whole is covered separately in [Macro Photography Basics: Magnification, Focus, and Light](/en/guides/macro-photography-basics).

The closer you get, the thinner the range in focus (the depth of field) becomes. If you calculate with a maximum magnification of 0.21x, F2.8, an APS-C sensor, and an assumed circle of confusion of 0.019mm, the depth that is in focus is only about 3mm. It is an approximate value I calculated myself, but it is clear that the closer you shoot, the more "where you place the focus" becomes the whole photo. The principle is explained in detail in [Why Macro Depth of Field Doesn't Behave Like Standard Depth of Field](/en/guides/macro-depth-of-field-vs-standard-dof).

## Blur the Background, Make the Plate Stand Out

All 7 photos in this post were taken at F2.8 and ISO 100. Opening the aperture fully makes the range in focus thin, so the pond and garden behind the plate turn into soft blobs of light. This is also why the outline of the fountain in the fried rice photos is blurred so that only its shape can be recognized. It is a way to keep the scenery in the background while letting the eye rest on the plate. How the range in focus changes is covered in [Understanding Depth of Field: Aperture, Focal Length, and Distance](/en/guides/understanding-depth-of-field).

Because it was a bright place, the shutter speed came out between 1/320 s and 1/500 s even at ISO 100 and F2.8. Food does not move, but when you shoot handheld from up close even small shake is magnified, so this shutter speed was a stable choice. What a bright lens changes in dark places is covered in [Why a Bright (Wide-Aperture) Lens Helps for Indoor Photography](/en/blog/indoor-photography-fast-aperture-lens).

Because this shoot was entirely at F2.8, I could not include a comparison between apertures. If you want the whole plate sharp, the usual approach is to stop down one or two stops and try around F4 or F5.6. The focus method also matters when shooting close, so see [Focus Modes Explained: AF-S vs AF-C vs Manual Focus](/en/guides/focus-modes-af-s-vs-af-c-vs-mf) as well.

## Checklist

- Did you check your lens's minimum focusing distance and maximum magnification in its specification sheet?
- Do you remember that the minimum focusing distance is measured from the sensor position (the ⌀ mark), not from the front of the lens?
- Before moving closer, did you try changing the angle three ways: low, at an angle, and from above?
- Did you decide which part of the plate to focus on (such as the nearest ingredient) before shooting?
- Is your lens or your body casting a shadow over the plate?

## Common Mistakes: Pushing Closer Than the Minimum Focusing Distance

The most common mistake is to keep moving in even though the lens cannot focus. When you get closer than a lens's minimum focusing distance, it will not focus and the camera hunts for focus. In that case, step back and find the point where focus locks, and if you want a larger image, it is better to crop the photo later. The next is going too close with a wide-angle lens so that the plate edge looks bent or the lens casts a shadow on the food. Using the longer end of the zoom range (35mm in this shoot) and shifting your position slightly solves most of it.

Shooting without deciding where to focus is another common mistake. At close distances where the depth of field becomes only a few millimeters, if focus lands behind the plate, the very part you most want to show becomes blurry. It is safer to use touch AF or single-point AF and assign focus to the nearest ingredient or the most striking part.

## FAQ

### Can I take close-up food photos without a macro lens?

Yes. These photos were taken with a wide-angle zoom lens without a macro function. If you get as close as the lens's minimum focusing distance, you can fill the frame with part of the plate. However, macro lenses often enlarge up to 1x (life size), so if you want to fill the frame with a very small subject, a dedicated lens is more advantageous.

### Where is the minimum focusing distance measured from?

It is measured from the position of the camera sensor, not from the front end of the lens. The ⌀-shaped mark on the top of the camera shows the sensor position. So the real gap between the front of the lens and the subject is shorter than the number in the specifications.

### Doesn't shooting food with a wide-angle lens cause distortion?

The closer you get and the wider the angle of view, the more the edges look stretched. These photos were taken at 26 to 35mm (about 42 to 56mm in full-frame equivalent), so the distortion was not noticeable. At the widest angle such as 15mm, getting right up to the plate can make distortion larger.

### What aperture is best for food photos?

If you want a blurred background, open the aperture, and if you want the whole plate sharp, stop down one or two stops. This shoot was entirely at F2.8, so the background blurred strongly, and in the photos taken close the range in focus was very thin.

## Putting It Together

If you divide food photography into full shots, middle shots, and close-ups, you can get several scenes from a single plate. This shoot confirmed that even without a dedicated macro lens, if you know the minimum focusing distance of the lens you own and get close within it, you can capture almost like a macro shot. The distance is measured from the sensor, and since the depth of field gets thinner as you get closer, it is important to decide the focus position first. Use the settings in this post as a reference and try shooting one plate at three distances and angles at your next meal.
