## The Short Answer: My Default Settings

| Setting | Range |
|---|---|
| ISO | 50 – 800 |
| Aperture | F8 – F11 |
| Shutter speed | 10 – 30 seconds |

Shooting within this range gave me nice starbursts. These values are not a fixed answer, though. Depending on how much light is around you, you need to **adjust shutter speed and ISO as needed**. The aperture controls the shape of the starburst, so keep it within F8–F11 and match brightness with shutter speed and ISO. That is the basic flow.

In practice I used ISO 100, F11, 30 seconds as the baseline in many shots, and dropped to ISO 50 depending on the scene. All the photos in this post were taken within this range. The EXIF information is written under each photo, and the same information is in the frame at the bottom of each photo.

## Why Do Light Starbursts Happen?

A light starburst (sunstar) is the effect where a point light source such as a street lamp or a light bulb appears to spread out like a star. When you stop the lens down, the opening inside the lens becomes a small polygon, and light diffracts at its edges, producing split rays of light.

The number of rays is determined by the number of aperture blades. If the blade count is odd, you get twice as many rays as blades; if it is even, you get the same number as blades. For example, the Canon RF70-200mm F2.8 L IS USM has 9 aperture blades, so in theory it produces 18 rays. Because the blade count differs from lens to lens, the same scene looks different depending on the lens.

## How to Adjust Shutter Speed and ISO for the Surrounding Brightness

Exposure is a balance of aperture × shutter speed × ISO. One step (1 stop) means the amount of light doubles or halves. Using F11, ISO 100, 30 seconds as the baseline, these combinations keep the same brightness:

| Aperture | ISO 100 | ISO 200 | ISO 400 | ISO 800 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F11 | 30 s | 15 s | 7.5 s | 3.75 s |
| F8 | 15 s | 7.5 s | 3.75 s | about 2 s |

If it is bright around you, lower the ISO or shorten the shutter speed; if it is dark, do the opposite. If the math is a hassle, the [Exposure Stop Calculator](/en/tools/exposure-stops-calculator) shows equivalent exposure combinations right away.

## Tips for Sharper Light Starbursts

1. **Aim for point light sources.** Starbursts are clearer from small, bright points such as street lamps, small bulbs, or the moon than from broad, diffuse lighting.
2. **Take off the UV filter and clean the lens.** Canon's guidance also recommends removing UV filters and keeping the lens clean. A filter can cause unwanted flare from reflections, and smudges make the rays look messy.
3. **A tripod is a must.** At 10–30 second exposures, any shake means a failed shot. Use a remote release or the self-timer too; see [How to Avoid Camera Shake in Long Exposures](/en/guides/avoiding-camera-shake-long-exposure) for details.

4. **Don't stop down too far.** The more you stop down, the longer and crisper the rays get, but image quality drops too because of diffraction. The Airy disk diameter is roughly 2.44 × wavelength × f-number; at 550 nm that is about 10.7 µm at F8, about 14.8 µm at F11, and about 21.5 µm at F16. For reference, the Canon EOS R7's pixel pitch is about 3.2 µm, so resolution loss can become noticeable at F16. That is why F8–F11 is the balance point between starbursts and sharpness. You can read more in [Diffraction and Optimal Aperture](/en/guides/diffraction-and-optimal-aperture) and the [Diffraction & DoF Calculator](/en/tools/advanced-dof-diffraction-calculator).

## FAQ

### Are light bloom and light starbursts the same thing?

They are different. A light starburst is the effect where a point light source throws star-shaped rays when you stop the lens down, and you can create it on purpose with the settings in this post. What people usually call light bloom more often means hazy glow around a strong light, or lens flare and ghosting, and it can also come from fingerprints, dust, or moisture on a filter or the lens surface, or from backlight. If you want rays, stop the aperture down; if you want less glow, start by cleaning the lens and filter and using a lens hood.

### Do light starbursts need a long shutter speed?

No. Starbursts come from stopping the aperture down, so they are not directly tied to shutter speed. However, stopping down lets in less light, so exposure times naturally get longer at night.

### What aperture is best?

In my experience, F8–F11 worked best. The rays show up well and image quality does not drop much. Lenses differ, so I recommend shooting your own tests and finding the value you like.

### Can I raise the ISO?

If it is dark around you, you can raise ISO up to 800 to shorten the shutter speed. Noise can increase in long exposures, so start with a low ISO if possible and raise it only as much as you need.

## Putting It Together

- The default settings are ISO 50–800, aperture F8–F11, and shutter speed 10–30 seconds.
