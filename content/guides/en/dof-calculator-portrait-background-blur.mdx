Shoot a headshot at f/1.8 and the background still looks distractingly sharp, and the instinct is to blame the lens or reach for an even wider aperture. Often the real culprit is distance and focal length, not the f-number on the barrel — and a depth of field calculator is the fastest way to see why before you waste a session finding out the hard way.

## Why aperture alone doesn't guarantee blur

Depth of field depends on four things at once: focal length, aperture, subject distance, and sensor size, combined through the hyperfocal distance formula (H = f² / (N·c) + f, where c is the circle of confusion for your sensor). Because focal length appears squared in that formula, it has an outsized effect — doubling the focal length shrinks the depth of field far more than opening up by a full stop does. That's the part a lot of "shoot wide open" advice skips over.

## A concrete comparison: 50mm vs. 85mm at the same distance

Shoot a half-body portrait at 2m on a full-frame body: 50mm at f/1.8 gives a total in-focus zone of about 17cm, while 85mm at f/1.8 from the same spot narrows that to about 6cm — nearly three times thinner, even though the aperture is identical. That's why an 85mm portrait lens has a reputation for creamier backgrounds than a 50mm at the same f-number: the extra focal length is doing more of the work than the aperture is.

## The surprising part: focal length matters less than you'd think once framing matches

Step back and compare framing instead of distance: to get the same head-and-shoulders crop, you'd shoot the 50mm from about 1m and the 85mm from about 2m. Run those numbers and the gap nearly disappears — around 4cm of depth of field at 50mm/1m versus about 6cm at 85mm/2m. Once distance is adjusted to match the composition, the background separation you associate with telephoto portrait lenses comes mostly from magnification — a background object appears larger and thus more visibly blurred at 85mm — not from a dramatically thinner focal plane. Knowing which effect you're actually chasing changes what gear or distance change is worth trying.

## Where razor-thin depth of field becomes a liability

Push the numbers further: 85mm at f/1.8 from just 1m for a tight headshot leaves a total in-focus zone of roughly 1.4cm. That's thinner than the distance from a subject's nose to their ears, so a small focus miss or the subject leaning forward mid-breath can leave the eyes soft while the eyebrows are sharp. In that situation, stopping down to f/4 at the same 2m distance widens the zone to about 13cm — still a clearly blurred background, with far more room for error on focus.

## Using the calculator before you shoot

ExifLens's Depth of Field (DoF) & Hyperfocal Distance Calculator (/tools/dof-calculator) takes your sensor format, focal length, aperture, and subject distance and returns the exact near and far limits — no guessing required. Before a shoot, plug in your planned distance and lens, and check where the far limit lands relative to your actual background. If your subject is 2m away and the background is a wall 3m behind them, a far limit of 2.03m puts that wall roughly a meter past the edge of focus, which is what guarantees the soft blur — a background only slightly closer than that might land inside the zone and stay distractingly sharp. Selecting your specific sensor preset (full-frame, APS-C, Micro Four Thirds) matters here too, since a smaller sensor's tighter circle of confusion changes the numbers even at identical focal length and aperture. For more on how the underlying near/far math works, see [our depth of field guide](/guides/understanding-depth-of-field).

## Field checklist

- To blur a background more, moving closer or using a longer focal length usually does more than opening the aperture another stop.
- At the same distance, doubling focal length shrinks the in-focus zone by roughly the square of that ratio — check it with the calculator rather than assuming.
- Once framing is matched, telephoto "bokeh" is driven mostly by background magnification, not by a dramatically thinner depth of field.
- Very close, very wide combinations (85mm, f/1.8, under 1.5m) can leave less than 2cm of focus — confirm your margin before shooting.
- Check the far limit against your actual background distance, not just against "infinity," before deciding the aperture is wide enough.
