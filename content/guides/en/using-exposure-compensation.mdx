If you've ever let the camera's own meter pick the exposure for a snowy ski slope and gotten back a flat, muddy gray field instead of bright white snow, that's not a lens or sensor problem — it's the meter doing exactly what it was built to do. Reflective light meters average everything in the frame and assume the scene sits at roughly 18% gray, a middle brightness. When the real scene is much brighter or darker than that assumption, the meter's "correct" exposure is wrong for what you're actually looking at. Exposure compensation is the dial that lets you override that assumption and tell the camera to render the scene brighter or darker than its own math suggests.

## Why the meter gets it wrong: the 18% gray assumption

Whether it's spot, center-weighted, or evaluative/matrix metering, every reflective meter measures light bouncing off the scene back into the lens, not the light actually falling on the subject. Because it has no way to know whether that reflected light is coming off snow, skin, or a black jacket, it falls back on a single assumption: that everything it measures averages out to roughly 18% gray, the tone used as a stand-in for typical midtone brightness. That assumption holds up well for an average outdoor scene with a mix of tones, which is why most daylight shots need no compensation at all. It falls apart the moment one tone dominates the frame, because the meter keeps trying to render that dominant tone as gray rather than as what it actually is.

## Correcting bright scenes: snow fields and white dresses

Point evaluative (matrix) metering at a snow-covered landscape and the meter tries to pull all that white down toward middle gray, so the shot comes out dark and dull. Dialing in +1 to +2 stops of positive compensation is usually what it takes to get the snow back to actual white. Say the meter suggests f/8, 1/500s, ISO 200 for the scene: +1 stop slows the shutter to 1/250s (or opens the aperture to f/5.6) to let in twice the light, and +2 stops gets you to 1/125s or f/4. A wedding dress filling most of the frame behaves the same way and typically needs +1 to +1.5 stops for the same reason.

## Correcting dark scenes: tuxedos and stage backdrops

The opposite happens when dark tones dominate the frame — a black tuxedo, a dark stage background, a black pet filling the shot. The meter tries to brighten all that dark tone toward middle gray, so the photo comes out lighter and muddier than it should. Dialing in roughly -1 stop restores the actual blacks. A common mistake at weddings is carrying the +1 stop compensation used for the bride's dress straight into photos of the groom's tuxedo, which then come out badly overexposed — the two scenes need compensation in opposite directions.

## Metering mode changes how much compensation you need

Evaluative/matrix metering, which averages the whole frame, is what produces the large compensation values above. Spot metering directly off a mid-tone area — skin in typical lighting, for instance — is already close to correct, so it needs little or no further compensation. Spot meter off an extreme tone instead, like a white shirt or a black jacket, and you'll need even more compensation than matrix metering would. Applying the same compensation value out of habit regardless of which metering mode is active is a fast way to get inconsistent results as the scene changes.

## Checking your compensation against the histogram

Dialing in a compensation value isn't the last step — check the histogram right after the shot for clipping at either wall. If +2 stops on that snow scene pushes the histogram against the right wall, you've blown out the texture in the snow entirely, and backing off to roughly +1.3 stops will usually bring it back. If -1 stop on the tuxedo shot piles data up against the left wall, you've lost shadow detail, and easing off to about -0.7 stops recovers it. Checking the histogram after every compensated shot, not just trusting the LCD preview, is what actually confirms the value you picked.

## The common mix-up: "exposure compensation doesn't work in Manual mode"

In full Manual mode with aperture, shutter speed, and ISO all set by hand, turning the exposure compensation dial does nothing to the actual brightness of the photo — it only moves the meter's needle, which leads a lot of people to conclude the dial is broken in M mode. It isn't: turn on Auto ISO alongside Manual mode, and the compensation dial works again, this time by pushing the camera's chosen ISO up or down. If you want to lock aperture and shutter speed yourself while still using the compensation dial, Auto ISO in Manual mode is the combination that makes that possible.

## Field checklist

- White dominating the frame (snow, a white dress, a white wall) usually needs +1 to +2 stops of positive compensation.
- Black dominating the frame (a tuxedo, a dark backdrop) usually needs around -1 stop of negative compensation.
- Spot-metering an extreme tone directly needs more compensation than evaluative/matrix metering of the same scene.
- Check both ends of the histogram after every compensated shot, not just the LCD preview.
- If the compensation dial seems to do nothing in Manual mode, check whether Auto ISO is switched off.
