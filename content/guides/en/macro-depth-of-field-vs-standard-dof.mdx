[Standard depth-of-field guidance](/guides/understanding-depth-of-field) — the kind built into most DoF calculators, including ours — assumes your subject is far enough from the lens that the focusing distance is much larger than the focal length. That assumption holds for almost every landscape, portrait, or street photo. It breaks down entirely once you're shooting close to 1:1 magnification, which is exactly where [macro depth of field](/guides/macro-photography-basics) already collapses to a few millimeters. The numbers a standard calculator gives you near 1:1 aren't just imprecise — the formula they're built on no longer describes what's actually happening.

## Standard DoF math depends on distance; macro DoF depends on magnification

A typical depth-of-field formula treats the near and far focus limits as a function of focal length, aperture, circle of confusion, and subject distance. In macro work, subject distance is only a few centimeters and magnification approaches or exceeds 1:1, so the depth of field is better described directly in terms of magnification and aperture rather than distance. Two lenses of very different focal lengths, both set to the same magnification and aperture, end up with almost the same depth of field — something the standard distance-based formula doesn't capture well at all.

## The near/far split flips

In normal photography, depth of field is asymmetric: roughly a third of it extends in front of the focus point and two-thirds behind it, which is exactly why the hyperfocal distance technique works — focusing slightly closer than infinity still keeps distant subjects sharp. Near 1:1 magnification, that asymmetry disappears. The near and far limits of macro depth of field split close to evenly, roughly half in front of the focus point and half behind. Applying the "focus a third of the way in" habit from landscape photography to a macro shot doesn't help the way it does at normal distances.

## How thin depth of field actually gets

At 1:1 magnification, even at f/11 the total depth of field on many lenses is well under a millimeter — sometimes closer to half a millimeter. This is why a subject as small as an insect's eye can be sharp while its antennae, only a few millimeters further away, are already visibly soft, no matter how carefully the aperture is chosen. Stopping down further does extend this a little, but as covered in [macro diffraction](/guides/macro-photography-diffraction-explained), stopping down at high magnification runs into diffraction much sooner, so there's a hard ceiling on how much a narrower aperture alone can help.

## What to use instead of a standard DoF calculator near 1:1

Since standard hyperfocal-based DoF numbers stop being reliable near 1:1, the more useful approach for planning a macro shot is to think directly in terms of magnification and aperture: at a given magnification, total depth of field scales roughly linearly with the f-number, so doubling the aperture number (say f/8 to f/16) roughly doubles the depth of field — while also pushing the effective aperture further into diffraction. Rather than relying on a distance-based calculator, macro shooters typically decide how many total planes of focus a subject needs and either accept a single thin plane, or plan a [focus-stacked sequence](/guides/focus-stacking-for-macro-photography) sized to the number of frames that depth actually requires.

## Checklist

- Standard DoF formulas assume subject distance is much larger than focal length — that assumption fails near 1:1 magnification.
- Macro depth of field depends mainly on magnification and aperture, not on subject distance or focal length the way normal DoF does.
- The DoF split becomes nearly symmetric near 1:1, unlike the front-third/back-two-thirds split you can rely on at normal distances.
- Expect total depth of field at 1:1 to be a fraction of a millimeter even at moderately narrow apertures.
- When a single frame can't cover the depth a subject needs, plan a focus-stacked sequence instead of trusting a standard DoF calculator's numbers near 1:1.
