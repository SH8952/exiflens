Choosing between AF-S, AF-C, and MF isn't really a question of "automatic versus manual." AF-S and AF-C are both autofocus, but they differ in whether the camera keeps recalculating focus while you hold the shutter halfway — and that one difference is why a mode that nails focus on a static scene can leave every frame slightly soft once the subject starts moving. Understanding what each mode actually does makes it obvious which one loses in a given situation.

## AF-S (Single-Shot AF): lock focus, then recompose

AF-S locks focus the instant you half-press the shutter, and holds that focus as long as you keep the button pressed, regardless of whether the subject or the camera moves afterward. This is what makes focus-and-recompose possible — you can focus on an eye with a center autofocus point, hold the half-press, reframe to your preferred composition, and fire. The catch is that any change in distance between camera and subject after that lock becomes a problem. Focus on a face at f/1.4 and then lean forward slightly while recomposing, and the extremely shallow depth of field can pull the eye entirely out of the focal plane before the shutter fires. Stop down past f/2.8 on a still subject and this error often disappears into the depth of field, but for shallow-depth portrait work, moving the AF point directly onto the eye is generally safer than focusing and recomposing.

## AF-C (Continuous AF): tracking for as long as you hold the button

AF-C keeps recalculating focus continuously for as long as you hold the shutter halfway, which is essential for wildlife or sports where the distance to the subject keeps changing — focus updates to match the distance at the moment the shutter actually fires, not the moment you first pressed the button. The condition for this to work is that the AF point has to stay on the subject the whole time. Let a bird fly across the frame and drift off the AF point onto open sky, and the camera briefly refocuses toward infinity or the background; even after the subject re-enters the AF point, the lag from that refocus often costs a handful of the most decisive frames. Subject recognition on modern mirrorless cameras (eye detect, animal detect) reduces this considerably, but at angles or lighting where recognition drops out, manually keeping the AF point on the subject still matters. Pairing AF-C with continuous burst shooting and separating focus onto a back-button (AF-ON) rather than the shutter button lets you toggle into an AF-S-like lock simply by lifting your thumb, which means one configuration can handle most situations without switching modes.

## Manual Focus: for the moments autofocus gives up

The situations that call for manual focus are fairly predictable. Shooting through glass often has autofocus latching onto reflections or smudges on the glass surface instead of the subject behind it; fog, dense netting, low-contrast walls or open sky with no clear edge to lock onto, and astrophotography aimed at faint point sources like stars all tend to send autofocus hunting back and forth without ever settling. In these cases, using live view magnification (paired with focus peaking if your camera supports it) and turning the focus ring by hand is usually both faster and more reliable than waiting on autofocus to give up. Manual focus is also the better choice for macro work — at magnifications where depth of field shrinks to a few millimeters, even the tiny amount of camera or subject movement that happens while autofocus is hunting can shift the entire focal plane. Locking the camera down on a tripod, focusing manually with precision, and firing with a remote release or timer avoids that shake entirely.

## The common mistake: staying in one mode for everything

The most frequent error is leaving a single focus mode set for every situation. Use AF-C on a still portrait and the tiny hand movement during the shutter press can trigger a focus recalculation that pulls a frame that would have been perfectly sharp in AF-S slightly out of true. Use AF-S on an unpredictable subject like a child or a pet and the gap between the half-press and the moment the shutter actually opens is often enough for the subject to have moved, producing a string of frames where focus consistently lands just behind where it should be. Deciding whether the subject will hold still, keep moving, or do something in between before you start shooting — and setting the mode accordingly — eliminates a large share of these misses.

## Practical checklist

- Use AF-S for portraits, landscapes, and still life, but move the AF point onto the eye directly rather than focusing and recomposing at shallow apertures.
- Use AF-C with continuous burst shooting, ideally paired with back-button focus, for sports, wildlife, and kids or pets in motion.
- Switch to MF with live view magnification for shooting through glass, low-contrast scenes, and faint point-light subjects like stars.
- For macro work, lock the camera on a tripod, focus manually, and trigger with a remote release or timer to eliminate shake.
- When a shot comes out softer than expected, check the recorded focus mode and AF point in the EXIF data before assuming the cause was something else.
