Street photography differs from most other genres in one basic way: the subject won't wait for the camera. Someone ducking out of an alley, a hand folding an umbrella at a crosswalk, an expression caught in a bus window — these moments are usually gone within a second or two. The goal with exposure here isn't precision, it's speed. Focus and exposure need to already be roughly right the instant the camera comes up, which means the settings have to be prepared before you ever raise it to your eye.

## Aperture: Zone Focusing to Remove the Guesswork

Relying on autofocus alone on the street often means the camera hunts for focus the instant a person enters the frame, and by the time it locks, the moment is gone. That's why many street photographers use zone focusing instead. Set a 35mm-equivalent lens to f/8 and pre-focus at 3 meters, for example, and the hyperfocal math puts everything from roughly 1.8 meters to infinity inside the depth of field. That means most of what happens near the 3-meter mark stays sharp without ever touching the focus ring again. Stopping down further to f/11 widens that zone even more, but on an overcast day or in a shaded alley, that extra depth comes at the cost of shutter speed or ISO headroom elsewhere.

A common mistake with zone focusing is setting the distance once and forgetting about it. A 3-meter zone set up on a wide avenue can put subjects outside the depth of field the moment you duck into a narrow alley where people are suddenly much closer. It pays to re-estimate the working distance every time the environment changes.

## Shutter Speed: The Floor for Freezing a Walking Pedestrian

- **Stationary subject, storefront scene**: 1/125s–1/250s
- **Person walking at a normal pace**: 1/250s–1/500s
- **Cyclist, runner, or passing traffic**: 1/500s–1/1000s

To freeze a person walking at a normal pace down to the wrists and feet, 1/500s is a safe target. At 1/250s, a torso-heavy composition usually still reads sharp, but if you want the swing of an arm or a stride to stay crisp too, it's worth the extra margin. Some street photographers go the other way and deliberately drop to 1/60s–1/125s, panning the camera with a walking subject to introduce a bit of motion blur that conveys speed. Either approach can work — what separates a strong frame from a mistake is whether the blur was intentional.

## Auto-ISO with a Minimum Shutter Speed Floor

Brightness on a street can swing dramatically in a handful of steps — walking from a sunlit avenue into a shadowed alley between buildings can shift exposure by three or four stops almost instantly. There's rarely time to manually adjust aperture or shutter speed for every change like that, so the most reliable setup in practice is to lock aperture and a minimum shutter speed and let ISO float automatically. Most cameras let you set that shutter speed floor inside auto-ISO — set it to match whatever your subject requires (1/500s for a walking pedestrian, say), and the camera will hold that speed and raise ISO instead. Skip that floor and the camera will often raise ISO only as much as it thinks is needed to prevent camera shake, which is a different problem than freezing a moving subject — and the result is frequently a photo blurred by the subject's own motion rather than a shaky hand.

## Handling Dynamic Range: Sun and Shadow in the Same Frame

The most common failure in midday street photography is letting an averaging meter blow out the highlights when a bright sidewalk and a dark shadow both sit inside the same frame. If a subject is walking out of shade into bright background light, switching to spot or partial metering and exposing for the bright area is the safer bet. Shooting RAW gives you real latitude to recover shadow detail afterward, but blown highlights are almost never recoverable. Dialing in exposure compensation of around -0.3 to -0.7 EV as a habit in bright scenes cuts down on highlight clipping before it happens.

## Settings for Shooting Discreetly

When photographing strangers, reducing the camera's presence matters almost as much as the exposure settings themselves. If your camera supports an electronic (silent) shutter, turning it on cuts down on the shutter sound drawing attention. Electronic shutter does have a tradeoff, though — fast-moving subjects or flickering artificial light can introduce rolling-shutter skew or banding, so it's worth testing a few frames before relying on it. Shooting through the viewfinder instead of the rear screen can also look more natural to people nearby than holding the camera out at arm's length, which sometimes draws less attention rather than more.

## Putting It Together

A practical starting point for midday street shooting: f/8–f/11 with zone focusing, auto-ISO with the minimum shutter speed locked to whatever your subject needs (1/500s for a walking pedestrian), and exposure compensation around -0.3 to -0.7 EV. A quick checklist before raising the camera: re-estimate your zone-focus distance for the space you just entered, confirm your auto-ISO shutter floor matches your subject's expected speed, switch to highlight-priority metering in bright scenes, and check a test frame for rolling-shutter artifacts if you're using an electronic shutter.
