If you've ever closed your aperture from f/4 to f/8 and watched the shot go dark, that wasn't a mistake on your part — it's proof that ISO, aperture, and shutter speed aren't three independent dials. Each one does a different job (sensor sensitivity, the size of the hole light passes through, and how long the sensor is exposed to it), but together they add up to a single number: the total amount of light that reaches the sensor. Move one, and one of the other two has to move too, or the exposure shifts.

## Stops: the shared currency

The reason three settings measured in totally different units — ISO as a plain number, aperture as an f-value, shutter speed in seconds — can be swapped for each other is that all three change the amount of light in the same unit: a stop, meaning a doubling or halving of light. Slow the shutter from 1/500s to 1/250s and you double the light (one stop). Open the aperture from f/8 to f/5.6 and you also double it (one stop). Raise ISO from 400 to 800 and the sensor's effective sensitivity doubles too (one stop). Because all three speak the same currency, a stop lost on one side can be paid back on either of the other two.

## A real example: matching exposure for a portrait on an overcast day

Say you're shooting a portrait outdoors on a cloudy afternoon and land on f/4, 1/500s, ISO 400 for a correct exposure. Then a kid starts running through the frame and you want to freeze the motion harder, so you bump the shutter to 1/2000s — four times faster, or two stops less light. You now need to find two stops somewhere else. You could open the aperture from f/4 to f/2 (two stops), or raise ISO from 400 to 1600 (two stops). Both land at the same final brightness, but the results look nothing alike: opening the aperture blurs the background further, while raising ISO keeps the background blur unchanged but adds visible noise to the image.

## Which corner to set first depends on the genre

The order in which you pick the three settings depends on what you're shooting. For fast subjects — sports, wildlife — lock shutter speed first (often 1/1000s or faster) and let the other two follow. Portrait photographers usually set aperture first (f/1.8–f/2.8) because it controls background blur, the thing they care about most. Landscape photographers narrow the aperture to around f/8–f/11 so the whole frame stays sharp, then balance shutter speed and ISO to match. If you have a tripod, you can push shutter speed as slow as you need and keep ISO pinned at 100; handheld, you generally need to respect a shutter-speed floor of roughly 1 over your lens's focal length in seconds to avoid camera shake, and that floor takes priority over everything else.

## The common mistake: chasing the lowest possible ISO no matter what

"Lower ISO is always better" is true in isolation, but photographers who apply it rigidly in low light — insisting on ISO 100–200 indoors — often end up with a shutter speed dragging down to 1/15s and a blurred frame. Motion blur can't be fixed in post; noise from a higher ISO usually can be reduced substantially with noise-reduction tools or careful editing. In dim conditions, accepting some noise to protect your shutter speed is almost always the better trade. The opposite mistake also happens: leaving Auto ISO's ceiling set too high (6400 or 12800), which lets the camera push ISO further than it needs to and quietly gives up an opportunity to use a more favorable aperture or shutter speed instead.

## Working the triangle through camera modes

Aperture Priority (Av/A) lets you fix the aperture and hands the camera the job of picking a matching shutter speed; Shutter Priority (Tv/S) does the reverse. Full Manual (M) means setting all three yourself, watching the meter to judge the result. Whichever mode you shoot in, the underlying math is the same — and opening the EXIF data on a shot afterward tells you exactly which combination the camera actually landed on, which makes it much faster to reproduce a look you liked the next time you're in a similar scene.

## Checklist

- In low light, a bit of noise beats a blurred frame — don't be afraid to raise ISO.
- Handheld, keep your shutter speed at or above 1 over your lens's focal length in seconds.
- Set aperture first when background blur matters most; set shutter speed first when freezing motion matters most.
- If you use Auto ISO, cap its ceiling deliberately for the conditions you're shooting in.
- When a shot turns out well, check its ISO, aperture, and shutter speed in the EXIF data so you can repeat the combination.
