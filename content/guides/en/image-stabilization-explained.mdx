A spec sheet claiming "5.5 stops of stabilization" makes it tempting to assume you can hand-hold a shot at 1/4s when the scene technically calls for 1/125s. Try it in practice, though, and more than half your frames at 1/4s often come out with a faint blur. The manufacturer isn't lying — the gap comes from how that number is measured versus how you actually shoot. Understanding what stabilization corrects and what it doesn't turns slow-shutter hand-holding from a guessing game into something predictable.

## IBIS and lens stabilization move different things

In-body image stabilization (IBIS) shifts the sensor itself in the opposite direction of detected shake to cancel it out. Lens-based stabilization (branded OIS, VR, IS depending on the maker) instead shifts an internal correction lens group to steady the optical path. Both rely on gyro sensors to detect movement and counteract it, but because they correct at different physical points in the system, they behave a little differently. Lens stabilization has the advantage of steadying the image you see through the viewfinder, which matters more as focal length gets longer and shake gets magnified, while IBIS works with any lens you mount, including old manual glass with no electronics at all. Many current cameras run both systems together as coordinated (dual) stabilization, which nets more usable stops than either one alone.

## The stop rating on the spec sheet comes from lab conditions

The stabilization figure manufacturers publish (5.5 stops, 8 stops, and so on) is measured under the CIPA standard — a controlled test using a specific lens on a rig or a trained tester holding a very stable stance, not the messy reality of a hand-held shot in the field. Real-world hand shake varies a lot by person, and factors like breathing, finger pressure on the shutter button, standing posture, or cold hands add variables the lab test never sees. That's why it's more realistic to assume you'll reliably get 3 to 4 stops out of a 5.5-stop rated system, not the full number. If your unstabilized safe shutter speed on a 50mm lens is 1/50s, trying to push all the way to 1/1.5s on faith in the spec is optimistic — shooting several frames around 1/3s to 1/6s (roughly 3.5 to 4 stops down) will get you a much higher keeper rate.

## Real scenario: calculating a safe hand-held shutter speed at night

Say you're hand-holding a 50mm lens indoors under soft ambient light to shoot a portrait. Without stabilization, the classic "1/focal length" rule puts your safe shutter speed around 1/50s. Apply a realistic 3.5-stop stabilization benefit and you can, in theory, push down to roughly 1/4s. But if your subject is moving even slightly, stabilization only cancels camera shake — it does nothing for subject motion blur — so the usable shutter speed drops back to something closer to 1/30s–1/60s. The key is to always separate "how much camera shake am I correcting" from "how much subject movement can I tolerate" when deciding how far to push a slow shutter.

## A common mistake: leaving stabilization on while mounted on a tripod

A surprisingly common mistake is leaving image stabilization active after the camera is already locked down on a tripod. Some older stabilization systems fail to recognize that the camera is completely still, mistake their own micro-vibrations for shake, and keep "correcting" against a movement that was never there — which can actually introduce blur into an otherwise stable shot. Many recent cameras auto-detect tripod use and disable stabilization on their own, but it's still safer to switch it off manually before a long exposure. This matters especially with ND filter long exposures running several seconds or more, where stabilization left on can be the very thing that softens an otherwise sharp frame.

## What image stabilization can't fix

Stabilization, true to its name, only cancels low-frequency hand shake. It does nothing for subject motion blur, a focus that was never accurate in the first place, or a hard jolt at the moment of the shutter press (like your whole body lurching as you mash the button). For fast-moving subjects — sports, wildlife — you still need a genuinely fast shutter speed; leaning on stabilization to drop the shutter speed in these situations just trades camera shake for a bigger dose of subject blur. Think of stabilization as a tool for reducing your own hand movement at slow shutter speeds, and handle subject motion and overall exposure as a separate decision.

## Checklist

- Treat the manufacturer's stop rating as a lab ceiling — plan around getting roughly 60-70% of it in real shooting conditions.
- Separate camera shake from subject motion blur before deciding how slow you can push the shutter speed.
- Switch stabilization off manually on a tripod or during long exposures, including ND filter shots.
- Remember that IBIS works with any mounted lens, while lens-based stabilization also steadies your viewfinder view.
- Fire off several frames at a slow shutter speed and pick the sharpest one rather than betting everything on a single shot.
