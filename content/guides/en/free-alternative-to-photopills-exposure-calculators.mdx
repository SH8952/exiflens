PhotoPills is one of the most established names in photography planning apps, known for its depth-of-field and exposure calculators, and especially its sun/moon/Milky Way planning tools with augmented-reality overlays. It's a paid, one-time-purchase mobile app for iOS and Android. If what you're looking for is specifically a free way to run core exposure and depth-of-field calculations in a browser, without installing an app or making a purchase, here's an honest look at where ExifLens overlaps with PhotoPills, and where it doesn't.

## Where the two overlap

ExifLens offers free, web-based calculators covering several of the same core categories PhotoPills is known for: depth of field, the exposure triangle, hyperfocal distance, bracketing/HDR exposure steps, astrophotography exposure via the NPF rule, and timelapse interval/storage planning. Because these run directly in a browser, there's nothing to install and no purchase required. That's useful if you want a quick calculation on a desktop, or on a phone you're not willing to install a paid app on.

## Where PhotoPills goes further

To be upfront about it: PhotoPills covers considerably more ground than a free web tool can reasonably match. Its augmented-reality sun, moon, and Milky Way direction planning, offline map-based shoot planning, and its own deeper feature layers (like Advanced DoF and Macro DoF) are built for a dedicated planning app and aren't something ExifLens's web calculators attempt to replace. If you regularly plan landscape or astro shoots around exact sun/moon positions at a specific location, PhotoPills' app-based approach is doing a job a browser calculator isn't designed for.

## Worked examples with the free tools

Here are five calculations we run all the time, with the arithmetic shown so you can check it by hand.

- **Hyperfocal distance** with the [DoF calculator](/tools/dof-calculator) or the [hyperfocal table](/tools/dof-hyperfocal-table): on a full-frame camera with a 24 mm lens at f/8 and a circle of confusion of 0.03 mm, the hyperfocal distance is 24 x 24 / (8 x 0.03) + 24 = 576 / 0.24 + 24 = 2,400 + 24 = 2,424 mm, about 2.4 meters. Focus there and everything from roughly 1.2 meters to infinity is acceptably sharp.
- **Long exposure with an ND filter** using the [exposure stops calculator](/tools/exposure-stops-calculator): a 10-stop filter multiplies exposure time by 2 to the power of 10, which is 1,024. A base exposure of 1/125 second becomes 1,024 / 125 = 8.2 seconds.
- **Bracketing** with the [bracketing calculator](/tools/bracketing-calculator): three frames at 2 EV spacing around a 1/60 second middle exposure give 1/250, 1/60, and 1/15 second, since each 2 EV step is a factor of four in time.
- **Timelapse** with the [timelapse calculator](/tools/timelapse-calculator): 240 frames played at 24 frames per second is 10 seconds of footage. Shot at a 5-second interval, that takes 240 x 5 = 1,200 seconds, or 20 minutes of shooting.
- **Storage** with the [storage calculator](/tools/storage-calculator): those 240 frames at 25 MB per raw file need 240 x 25 = 6,000 MB, about 6 GB.

For night sky work, the [astrophotography calculator](/tools/astrophotography-calculator) applies the NPF rule, and for planning light by time of day, the [golden hour calculator](/tools/golden-hour-calculator) gives sunrise, sunset, and blue hour windows from a date and location.

## A practical scenario walk-through

Imagine we are heading out for a waterfall shoot and want silky water in daylight.

1. We check the light with the [Sunny 16 calculator](/tools/sunny-16-calculator) for a starting exposure.
2. We decide how much we need to slow the shutter, then use the [exposure stops calculator](/tools/exposure-stops-calculator) to see how many stops of ND that equals.
3. We check depth of field for the foreground rocks with the [DoF calculator](/tools/dof-calculator), and confirm any diffraction softness at small apertures with the [advanced DoF and diffraction calculator](/tools/advanced-dof-diffraction-calculator).
4. We use the [camera FOV calculator](/tools/camera-fov-calculator) and the [crop factor calculator](/tools/crop-factor-calculator) to compare lens choices between bodies.

All of this happens in a browser tab, with no installation. What we do not get here is an augmented-reality view of where the sun will be at the waterfall, so for that part of the plan we would use a map, a compass, or a dedicated planning app.

## Common mistakes when comparing tools

- **Comparing apples to oranges.** A calculator and a full planning app solve different problems. Judge each by the job you need done.
- **Trusting any calculator's output without understanding the inputs.** The circle of confusion, the sensor size, and the focal length all change the answer. Always enter your real camera data.
- **Assuming a free tool is a lesser tool.** For a single calculation, a free browser calculator can be just as accurate as an app.
- **Assuming a paid app is overkill, or essential.** It depends on how often you plan shoots around exact celestial positions.

## Limits: when ExifLens is not the right choice

If you need live augmented-reality overlays, offline use in remote areas, or tight integration with a map of your exact shooting spot, a dedicated mobile planning app is the more suitable choice, and that applies to any app of this kind, not only one name. A browser calculator also depends on you entering correct values. It will not warn you if you mix up a crop sensor with a full-frame one.

## FAQ

**Can I use both?**
Yes. Many photographers keep a browser calculator for quick checks at a desk and use a planning app in the field.

**Do ExifLens calculators need an account?**
No. They run in your browser without sign-up.

**Is the hyperfocal result exact?**
It is a standard formula and a convention for sharpness, so it is a practical guide. If you plan a large print, use a stricter circle of confusion.
