A camera sensor can't record as much dynamic range as the human eye. Expose for a bright sky and the foreground rocks and trees fall into black; expose for the foreground and the sky blows out to white. A graduated ND (GND) filter solves this in-camera by darkening only half the glass, cutting light from the sky while leaving the foreground untouched.

## When you actually need one: measuring the gap

Before reaching for a filter, spot-meter the sky and the foreground separately. If the sky reads 1/250s and the foreground reads 1/15s, that's roughly a 4-stop gap. Exposure bracketing or HDR blending can close a gap that size too, but moving clouds or breaking waves make multi-frame blends messy. That's exactly where a GND earns its keep — it lets one shutter press expose both zones correctly at once.

## Soft edge, hard edge, reverse — pick by terrain, not preference

The transition shape should match your horizon, not your taste. A jagged skyline of mountains or buildings will show an unnatural dark band from a hard-edge filter wherever a peak or rooftop crosses the line, so a soft edge spreads that transition until it's invisible. A flat horizon — ocean, plains — is the opposite case: a hard edge keeps the transition narrow and lets you push full strength right up to the line where sky meets foreground. A reverse GND is darkest at the dead center and lightens toward both edges, built specifically for sunrise and sunset shots where the brightest point of the frame sits right at the horizon rather than at the top.

## Choosing stop strength: 0.6, 0.9, 1.2

GNDs are usually labeled 0.3 (1 stop), 0.6 (2 stops), and 0.9 (3 stops). For the 4-stop example above, a single 0.9 filter brings the gap down to about 1 stop — small enough to finish off with ordinary white balance and tone adjustments in post. When the gap runs 5-6 stops or more, in extreme backlit scenes, some photographers stack a 0.6 and a 0.9 together, but two filters stacked increases color cast and vignetting risk, especially on wide lenses. Bracketing exposures instead is often the safer route at that point.

## Aligning the transition line in a slot holder

Because a screw-in circular filter can't slide, GNDs almost always live in a slot (holder) system. In practice: lock focus and composition first, with no filter attached, and set your aperture. Then, with live view on, slide the filter up and down in the holder until the transition line sits exactly on the horizon — zooming into the live view display makes small misalignments much easier to catch. Setting the line a touch lower than the horizon itself lets the darkened band overlap the very top of the foreground slightly, which reads as a smoother, more natural blend than a razor-sharp cutoff.

## Common mistakes

The most common one is centering the line mechanically on the middle of the frame and calling it done. Any subject poking above the horizon — a mountain peak, a lighthouse, a tall tree — gets darkened along with the sky, which looks wrong. Switching to a soft edge, or accepting the darkening and locally brightening that subject in post, both work better than forcing a hard edge across an uneven skyline. On wide-angle lenses, a small shift in filter position changes a lot within the frame, so always zoom into the final image to confirm the line landed where you intended.

## Can digital blending replace it?

Shooting RAW and pulling down the sky with a graduated mask in post can approximate the same result, and plenty of photographers skip the physical filter entirely for that reason. The catch is that highlights already clipped on the sensor can't be recovered afterward — no amount of post-processing brings back detail that was never captured. When the gap is wide enough to blow the sky to pure white, physically cutting the light at capture time still gives more room to work with than digital blending alone, which is the real argument for keeping a filter in the bag.

## Field checklist

- Spot-meter sky and foreground separately before picking a filter strength.
- Uneven horizon: soft edge. Flat horizon: hard edge.
- A 3-4 stop gap is usually solved by a single 0.9 filter.
- Lock focus and composition without the filter, then align the line using live view.
- Zoom into the final frame to check that nothing poking above the horizon got darkened unnaturally.
