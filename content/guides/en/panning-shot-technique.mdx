A panning shot is one of the few techniques where a slow shutter speed and a sharp subject aren't a contradiction. By moving the camera at the same angular speed as a moving subject during a longer exposure, the subject stays relatively still on the sensor while everything else streaks — background, foreground, even the ground the subject is moving across. Done well it reads as motion in a way a frozen 1/2000s frame never does. Done poorly it just looks like a missed shot, and the gap between the two comes down to a handful of settings and a repeatable body technique.

## Shutter Speed: Slow Enough to Blur, Fast Enough to Track

The core trade-off in panning is shutter speed. Too fast and the background barely blurs, so the shot loses its sense of motion; too slow and your panning motion can't stay matched closely enough to the subject's speed for the whole exposure, so the subject itself softens. For a cyclist or jogger moving at a moderate pace across the frame, 1/30s to 1/60s is a reasonable starting point. A car or motorcycle moving faster, especially close to the camera, often needs 1/125s to 1/250s just to keep the subject itself sharp, even though that's fast enough that some photographers assume it won't produce enough background blur — in practice it usually does, because the background is moving past the frame at a much higher relative speed than the subject is moving past your eye.

- **Walking or slow jogging subject**: 1/15s–1/30s
- **Cyclist or running athlete**: 1/30s–1/60s
- **Car, motorcycle, or subject close to the lens**: 1/125s–1/250s

A common mistake is picking one shutter speed for an entire session regardless of subject distance. A cyclist crossing the frame ten meters away needs a noticeably slower shutter speed to produce the same background streak length as the same cyclist crossing thirty meters away, because angular speed relative to the lens drops with distance even if the physical speed doesn't change.

## Tracking the Subject: Smooth, Continuous, From the Hips

The panning motion itself matters as much as the shutter speed. Rotating from the waist or hips, rather than just swinging the arms or wrists, produces a smoother arc and avoids the small vertical wobble that shows up as a soft or double-exposed subject even when the shutter speed was correct. A useful setup is to pre-aim at the point where the subject will enter the frame, start rotating your body before pressing the shutter so you're already moving at the subject's speed when the exposure begins, and continue the same rotation through and past the shot rather than stopping the moment the shutter closes — stopping early is one of the most common causes of a soft subject, since the last fraction of the exposure catches a camera that's already decelerating.

Continuous autofocus (AF-C) with subject or eye tracking, if available, keeps the focus point locked to the subject as it crosses the frame rather than to whatever was under the focus point when you pressed the shutter. Back-button focus paired with continuous release makes it easier to keep refining focus through the pan without accidentally triggering focus lock on a half-press.

## Aperture and ND Filters: Getting to a Usable Shutter Speed

Panning shots are usually taken in daylight, which is exactly when a slow shutter speed like 1/30s tends to overexpose even at a narrow aperture and low ISO. Stopping down to f/16 or f/22 buys some room, but diffraction softens fine detail past a lens's optimal aperture, and panning shots already have enough motion softness that adding diffraction blur on top rarely helps the final image. A 2- to 3-stop ND filter is usually a cleaner solution: it lets you keep the aperture in a sharper range (commonly f/8–f/11) while still reaching a shutter speed slow enough for the background to streak.

## Common Failure Modes and How to Read Them

A subject that's soft everywhere, background included, usually means the shutter speed was too slow for how well the pan tracked the subject's actual speed — either the subject accelerated mid-pan or the panning motion wasn't smooth. A subject that's sharp but the background barely blurs usually means the shutter speed was too fast for the subject's speed and distance combination. A subject with a faint double-edge or ghosting, most visible around moving legs or wheels, often comes from a flash or a rolling shutter interaction rather than the pan itself, and is worth checking the EXIF for flash-fired status if it shows up consistently.

## Putting It Together

A practical starting point for a cyclist or runner in daylight: shutter priority at 1/30s–1/60s, continuous autofocus with subject tracking, aperture wherever the metering lands (adding an ND filter if it's forcing you past f/16), and a smooth waist-level rotation that starts before the shutter and continues after it. Checklist before a session: confirm AF-C and continuous shooting are active, take a few test frames to check the actual shutter speed against the subject's approach speed, watch the EXIF of your sharpest test frame to lock in that shutter speed rather than guessing, and practice the follow-through rotation on a few subjects before the moment that matters.
