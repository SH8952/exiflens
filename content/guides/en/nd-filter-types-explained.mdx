Choosing an ND filter isn't just about picking a strength (covered in our guide to [choosing the right ND filter](/guides/choosing-the-right-nd-filter)) — you also have to decide on a physical format. The format affects cost, convenience, and which lenses you can even use it on. Here's what's actually available.

## Screw-On (Circular) Filters

A screw-on ND filter threads directly onto the front of your lens, matching its filter thread diameter (58mm, 77mm, 82mm, and so on). It's the simplest, most portable option, and generally the least expensive way to get started.

The catch is that a filter sized for one lens often doesn't fit another. Photographers who own several lenses either buy a separate filter per thread size, or buy filters sized for their largest lens and use step-up rings to adapt them to smaller lenses. Screw-on filters can also be slow to swap in changing light, since you're physically threading and unthreading glass each time.

## Square and Slot Filter Systems

A slot (or "holder") system uses a bracket that mounts to the front of the lens, into which flat, square or rectangular filters slide. Because the holder — not the filter — is what's matched to the lens thread, one set of filters can be shared across every lens you own, provided you have the right adapter ring for each.

Slot systems are the standard choice for landscape photographers who use graduated filters (below), since sliding a filter up or down in the holder lets you position the transition line exactly on the horizon — something a fixed circular filter can't do. The tradeoff is bulk: a filter holder plus a set of square filters is heavier and slower to set up than a screw-on filter, and better suited to deliberate tripod work than handheld shooting.

## Solid ND vs Graduated ND

This is a separate axis from screw-on vs. slot — both formats come in solid and graduated versions.

**Solid ND** filters are uniformly tinted across the entire piece of glass or resin — the type covered in our filter-strength guide, used to slow down the whole exposure evenly regardless of what's in the frame.

**Graduated ND (GND)** filters are only tinted on part of the filter, transitioning to clear glass toward the other edge. They're built to darken a bright sky while leaving a darker foreground untouched, compressing a scene's dynamic range in-camera rather than relying entirely on post-processing. Graduated filters come in a few transition styles:

- **Soft-edge**: a gradual transition, forgiving of an uneven horizon (trees, mountains, a skyline).
- **Hard-edge**: a sharp transition, best for a flat, clean horizon like open ocean.
- **Reverse**: darkest right at the center of the filter, fading toward both the top and bottom — built for sunrise and sunset, where the brightest point of the sky sits right at the horizon rather than at the top of the frame.

## Which Format Should You Buy First?

If you're just getting into long exposure and own one or two lenses, a screw-on solid ND — most photographers start with a 6-stop or 10-stop — is the simplest, cheapest entry point. If you shoot landscapes seriously across several lenses and regularly deal with bright skies over darker foregrounds, a slot system with both a solid ND and a soft-edge graduated ND covers the two most common needs without needing four or five separate screw-on filters.

## A Note on Quality

Cheap ND filters, in either format, are prone to a color cast — most often a magenta or blue tint — that gets more pronounced at higher stop counts. It shows up clearly when checking a photo's white balance after the fact, and it's worth correcting for or avoiding by choosing filters from manufacturers known for neutral color rendition, especially for strong 10-stop-and-above filters where the effect is hardest to correct convincingly in post-processing.
