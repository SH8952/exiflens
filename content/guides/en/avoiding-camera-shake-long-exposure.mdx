Long exposure photography turns camera shake from a minor annoyance into the single most common reason a shot fails. At a shutter speed of 1/200s, small vibrations barely register; at 10 seconds, they're stretched across the entire frame and show up as soft, smeared detail that no amount of sharpening can fully recover. Most of the fixes are cheap and mechanical, not expensive gear.

This guide covers the seven things that cause blur in long exposures, in the order you will usually meet them, plus a few real examples of sharp night shots taken on a tripod with their exact settings.

## Why It Matters More at Slow Shutter Speeds

Any movement that happens while the shutter is open gets recorded as part of the exposure. A fast shutter speed freezes vibration because it's over before the movement has time to travel far; a slow shutter speed has nowhere to hide it. This is why a setup that looks perfectly stable to the eye can still produce a soft image: vibrations too small to see or feel are often large enough to blur a 10-second exposure.

## The 7 Causes and Their Fixes at a Glance

| Cause of blur | What you see | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Flimsy tripod setup | Soft detail everywhere, worse at the edges | Extend thick leg sections first, keep the center column low |
| Pressing the shutter button | Slight softness or a double edge | Remote release or 2-second self-timer |
| Mirror slap or shutter shock | Soft in a narrow shutter-speed range | Mirror lock-up, electronic first curtain, or electronic shutter |
| Wind on the camera | Streaks that point in one direction | Lower the tripod, spread the legs, add weight, block the wind |
| Ground vibration | Soft frames when something passes by | Avoid bridges, docks and decks, wait between passing vehicles |
| Image stabilization on a tripod | Faint blur from a "perfect" setup | Switch stabilization off for tripod work |
| Focus drift | Sharp in the wrong place | Use manual focus after focusing once and check at 100% |

## Tripod Fundamentals

A stable tripod is the foundation, but "stable" means more than just standing upright. Extend the thicker upper leg sections before the thinner lower ones, since thinner sections flex more under load. Skip the center column extension if you can, or keep it as short as possible, since it's typically the least rigid part of the whole setup. On soft ground (sand, mud, snow), press the legs down firmly to seat them rather than letting them balance on the surface. If your tripod has a hook under the center column, hanging a weight (a bag, a water bottle) from it adds stability, especially in wind. For a longer discussion of what to look for when buying one, see our guide to [choosing a tripod](/en/guides/how-to-choose-a-tripod).

## Mirror and Shutter Vibration

DSLRs have a mechanical mirror that flips out of the way before each shot, and that motion alone can introduce a small amount of shake, especially in the fraction of a second right after the mirror moves. Mirror lock-up (or an electronic first curtain shutter mode, where available) separates the mirror flip from the actual exposure, giving vibrations time to settle before the shutter opens. Mirrorless cameras don't have this specific issue but can have their own shutter-shock equivalent at certain shutter speeds, which an electronic shutter mode eliminates entirely.

## Remote Triggering

Physically pressing the shutter button transmits a small amount of shake directly into the camera, and at long shutter speeds that's often enough to soften the shot. A wired or wireless remote avoids touching the camera at all. Without one, your camera's 2-second self-timer works nearly as well, because it gives any vibration from pressing the button time to die out before the exposure actually starts.

## Wind and Ground Vibration

A tripod eliminates hand shake but not external vibration. Wind pushing against the camera and lens, especially with a large lens or a lens hood acting like a sail, can shake the whole setup enough to blur a long exposure. Positioning yourself to block wind with your body, keeping the tripod low and legs spread wide for a lower center of gravity, and avoiding surfaces that transmit vibration (a wooden dock with foot traffic, a bridge with passing cars) all help. On a windy day, a heavier tripod genuinely outperforms a lighter one.

## Image Stabilization: Turn It Off

This one surprises people: in-lens or in-body image stabilization, designed to counteract handheld shake, can actually introduce blur when the camera is on a stable tripod. Some stabilization systems, with nothing to correct, generate small movements of their own trying to detect motion that isn't there. Most manufacturers recommend disabling stabilization for tripod work; check your specific system, since some newer models detect tripod use automatically and don't have the problem. For how stabilization works and how much you can trust it when hand-holding, read [Image Stabilization Explained](/en/guides/image-stabilization-explained).

## How to Diagnose a Blurry Long Exposure

If a frame comes out soft, the pattern of the blur points to the cause:

- **Everything is soft in the same way:** tripod flex, mirror or shutter vibration, or the shutter button press.
- **Streaks that all point the same direction:** wind or a vibration source like a passing truck.
- **Sharp center but soft edges:** usually the lens, aperture or focus plane, not shake. Stop down and check at 100%.
- **Only the moving subject is blurred:** that is the point of a long exposure. Nothing is wrong.
- **Blur only when stabilization is on:** the stabilization system is hunting. Switch it off on the tripod.

Always zoom in to 100% on the screen to judge sharpness. A frame can look fine on the camera's small display and still be soft.

## A Quick Pre-Shot Checklist

Before triggering a long exposure, run through it: tripod legs seated firmly on solid ground, center column kept short, mirror lock-up or electronic shutter enabled, remote trigger or self-timer set, image stabilization off, and a body position that blocks wind if it's present. It takes a few seconds and eliminates the majority of long-exposure blur before it ever happens.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What shutter speed is too slow to hand-hold?

A common starting point is 1 divided by your focal length in seconds, adjusted for your crop factor. Stabilization can add a few stops, but it never helps with subject movement. See [Image Stabilization Explained](/en/guides/image-stabilization-explained) for a calculation table.

### Do I need a remote release for long exposures?

It is the best option, but the 2-second self-timer works almost as well. The goal is to keep your hand off the camera while the exposure starts.

### Should image stabilization be on or off on a tripod?

Off, unless your camera detects tripod use automatically. Some systems can introduce a faint blur when they try to correct motion that isn't there.

### Does a heavier tripod really make a difference?

On a windy day, yes. A heavier tripod resists wind better, and hanging a bag from the center hook adds the same stability to a lighter one.
