A focal length in millimeters doesn't tell you how wide a shot will actually be until you know the sensor it's paired with — the same 35mm lens frames a noticeably different scene on a full-frame body than on a Micro Four Thirds one. Our [Camera Field of View calculator](/tools/camera-fov-calculator) converts focal length and sensor size directly into an angle of view in degrees, which is the more universal way to describe how wide a lens actually sees.

## How to use it

Pick your sensor size (or enter custom width/height for a specific camera), then enter the focal length you're planning to shoot at. The calculator returns three numbers:

- **Horizontal FOV** — how wide the frame is, based on your sensor's width.
- **Vertical FOV** — how tall the frame is, based on your sensor's height.
- **Diagonal FOV** — the single number most often quoted for a lens's overall angle of view, based on the sensor's diagonal measurement, since it doesn't depend on whether you're shooting in landscape or portrait orientation.

Below that, a table shows the same three values across common focal lengths (14mm through 300mm) for your selected sensor, so you can compare a handful of lenses or zoom positions at once without re-entering focal length one at a time.

## Where this is useful beyond photography

Field of view in degrees is the format used in fields outside general photography where "what focal length is that" doesn't mean much on its own — surveying and mapping, videography and gimbal/drone work, and architectural or interior photography where you need to know in advance whether a lens will fit a whole room or facade into frame from a fixed shooting distance. If you already know the distance to your subject, you can combine the FOV angle with basic trigonometry to estimate how wide an area the frame will cover at that distance.
