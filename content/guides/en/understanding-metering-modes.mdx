Every camera has a light meter constantly measuring the scene in front of it, and metering mode determines exactly how it reads that light — which parts of the frame it weighs, and how heavily. Two photographers pointing the same camera at the same scene can get different exposure recommendations purely because their metering modes disagree about what matters in the frame.

## What a Light Meter Is Actually Doing

A camera's meter doesn't know what a "correctly exposed" photo of your specific scene should look like; it works from an assumption that the scene, averaged out, is roughly middle gray, and it recommends an exposure that would render that average as middle gray. This assumption holds up reasonably well for typical scenes and breaks down for anything unusually bright or dark overall — a snow scene, a black cat, a stage lit mostly by darkness with one bright spotlight — which is exactly where understanding your metering mode starts to matter.

## Matrix (or Evaluative) Metering

Matrix metering — called Evaluative on some brands — divides the frame into multiple zones, measures each one, and combines them using an algorithm that also considers focus point, color, and sometimes scene recognition. It's the default on most cameras and the right choice for the majority of everyday shooting, because it's built to handle typical scenes without you needing to think about metering at all. Where it struggles is exactly the situations described above: scenes that are legitimately very bright or very dark overall, where its assumption of "average equals middle gray" doesn't hold.

## Center-Weighted Metering

Center-weighted metering measures the whole frame like Matrix does, but gives much heavier weight to the center, on the assumption that your main subject is usually there. It's a reasonable middle ground for portraits and similar subject-centered shots where you want the exposure driven mainly by the subject rather than by a busy or unevenly lit background, without going as narrow as spot metering.

## Spot Metering

Spot metering ignores almost the entire frame and measures only a small area, usually tied to your focus point. This gives you precise control over exactly which part of the scene the camera treats as the reference for correct exposure, which matters a great deal in high-contrast situations. A backlit subject, a scene with the sun in frame, or a stage performer surrounded by darkness are all cases where averaging the whole frame (Matrix or Center-Weighted) would badly misjudge the exposure, while spot metering off the subject's face gives you exactly the reading you actually need.

## Choosing a Mode in Practice

Leave Matrix on as your default for general shooting — it genuinely handles most scenes well and second-guessing it constantly isn't a good use of attention. Switch to Center-Weighted for portraits where you want the subject prioritized over a distracting background. Switch to Spot for high-contrast situations — backlighting, spotlit subjects, sunrise and sunset shots with the sun in frame — where you need the exposure locked to one specific part of the scene rather than an average of the whole thing. Knowing which mode you're in, and why, turns metering from a mystery the camera handles for you into a tool you're actively using.

## Worked Example: Fixing Snow and Black Cats

Exposure compensation is simple arithmetic, because each stop doubles or halves the light. Say the meter reads a sunlit snowfield at f/8, 1/500s, ISO 100. The meter believes the snow is middle gray, so it underexposes it and the snow looks dull gray. Dial in +1 EV and the shutter moves to 1/250s: twice the light, and the snow renders much closer to white. Many scenes need between +1 and +2 EV, so check the result rather than trusting a fixed number.

The black cat is the mirror image. In a dim room the meter suggests f/2.8, 1/60s, ISO 1600 and tries to turn the cat gray. Dial in minus 1 EV and the shutter becomes 1/125s, which keeps the fur dark and also helps freeze motion. If you want to explore these combinations, the [exposure stops calculator](/tools/exposure-stops-calculator) does the doubling and halving for you.

## Scenario: Backlit Portrait

We place a subject in front of a bright sky. With Spot metering on the sky, the camera might read f/5.6, 1/2000s. With Spot metering on the face, it reads f/5.6, 1/250s. Going from 1/2000s to 1/250s is a ratio of 8, which is three stops, so the face is three stops darker than the sky. Matrix metering averages these two readings and typically leaves the face too dark. Spot on the face gives a good portrait and a sky that is blown out, which is often an acceptable trade. If you want both, shoot a series and merge it, and the [bracketing calculator](/tools/bracketing-calculator) can help plan the spacing between frames.

## Common Mistakes

- **Spot metering a white shirt or a black jacket.** The meter will turn it middle gray. Meter a mid-tone such as skin or grass, or compensate by hand.
- **Forgetting to reset exposure compensation.** A leftover +1 EV from the snow session ruins the next indoor shoot.
- **Switching modes mid-scene without thinking.** Change modes between scenes, not between frames.
- **Metering where the focus point is without realizing it.** On many cameras Spot follows the focus point, so a recomposed shot meters from a different place than you intended.

## When This Doesn't Apply

Metering modes only matter when the camera is choosing the exposure for you. In full Manual mode with a fixed ISO, the mode merely changes the indicator needle. Flash exposure uses its own pre-flash measurement, so the mode has little effect on the flash output. For deliberate effects such as silhouettes or low-key images, your own judgment overrides the meter. And a very bright or very dark scene can fool any mode, so a histogram check afterwards is still wise.

## Quick Checklist

- Start in Matrix and review the first shot
- Switch to Spot when the subject and background differ by several stops
- Add plus EV for bright scenes and minus EV for dark ones
- Meter a mid-tone, or compensate for white and black subjects
- Reset compensation when the scene changes

## FAQ

### Which metering mode is best for beginners?
Matrix. It handles most scenes well, and the habit of adjusting exposure compensation afterwards teaches the most useful skill.

### Is Spot metering the same as exposure lock?
No. Spot decides which area is measured, while exposure lock (AE-L) freezes the reading so you can recompose. The two are often used together.

### Why is my photo too dark even in Matrix mode?
The scene is probably bright overall, or the subject is darker than the background. Try plus EV, or switch to Spot on the subject.
